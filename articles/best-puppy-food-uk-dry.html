The best puppy food in the UK will depend on your dog's breed size, age, and any sensitivities, but as a starting point, look for a complete food with named meat as the first ingredient, appropriate calcium-to-phosphorus ratios for growth, and a FEDIAF-compliant nutritional guarantee. Whether you choose dry kibble, wet food, or raw, the fundamentals are the same: your puppy needs more protein, more calories, and more carefully balanced minerals than an adult dog, because their bones, muscles, and organs are still forming rapidly in those first months of life.

In this guide, we have compared the most popular and well-regarded puppy foods available in the UK in 2026, covering dry, wet, raw, and fresh options. We have looked at ingredient quality, nutritional completeness, price per day, and real owner feedback, so you can feed your new arrival with genuine confidence rather than guesswork.

What to Look for in a Good Puppy Food?

A good puppy food must be nutritionally complete for growth, which means it meets the minimum nutrient profiles set by FEDIAF (the European Pet Food Industry Federation), the body whose guidelines UK manufacturers follow post-Brexit. This is distinct from a "complementary" food, which is designed to be fed alongside something else. Always check the label for the phrase "complete food for puppies" or "complete food for all life stages."

Named meat as the first ingredient

The ingredient list on pet food is displayed by weight before processing. Loyal & Loved found that foods listing a named protein source (such as "chicken" or "salmon") rather than a vague term like "meat and animal derivatives" tend to offer more consistent nutritional quality and greater transparency about what your puppy is actually eating. According to a 2023 review published in the journal Animals, protein source specificity is positively associated with amino acid completeness in commercial pet foods.

Correct calcium and phosphorus levels

Puppies need a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of roughly 1.2:1 to 1.4:1 according to FEDIAF 2022 nutritional guidelines. Too much calcium, particularly in large and giant breed puppies, is linked to developmental orthopaedic disease including osteochondrosis. This is why large-breed-specific puppy foods exist and why feeding an adult food (which typically contains less calcium) to a large puppy is equally problematic. As Loyal & Loved explains, getting this ratio right is one of the most important and most overlooked aspects of puppy nutrition.

DHA for brain and eye development

Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an omega-3 fatty acid found in fish oil and fish-based ingredients, supports neurological and visual development in puppies. FEDIAF recommends a minimum of 0.05% DHA on a dry matter basis for puppy foods. Check ingredient lists for fish oil, salmon oil, or herring, which are common DHA sources in UK puppy foods.

Size-appropriate formulation

Small breeds (under 10 kg adult weight) mature faster and have higher metabolic rates, so they need more calorie-dense food in smaller kibble sizes. Large breeds (over 25 kg adult weight) grow for longer and need controlled energy and calcium levels to protect developing joints. Loyal & Loved found that using a breed-size-specific formula from a reputable manufacturer reduces the risk of both underfeeding and growth-related skeletal problems.

What to avoid

  • Artificial colours and flavours, which serve no nutritional purpose
  • High sugar or salt content, common in lower-quality wet foods
  • Unnamed "derivatives" as the primary protein source in budget foods
  • Excessive filler grains such as maize or corn syrup in the first three ingredients
  • Any food labelled "complementary" used as a sole diet

Dry puppy food (kibble) is the most popular format in the UK because it is convenient, cost-effective, and easy to measure accurately for portion control. The best dry puppy foods combine high-quality protein sources with carefully balanced minerals and tend to have a longer shelf life than wet or raw alternatives.

A note on grain-free dry puppy food

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) investigated a potential link between grain-free diets high in legumes and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM, a serious heart condition) in dogs from 2018 onwards. As of 2026, the FDA investigation remains open and no definitive causal link has been proven, but many UK vets advise caution with grain-free foods for puppies in breeds with known cardiac sensitivities. Speak to your vet before choosing a grain-free formula for your puppy, particularly if you have a Golden Retriever, Cocker Spaniel, or other breed with elevated DCM risk.

Best Wet Puppy Foods for Young Dogs

Wet puppy food (food with a moisture content above 60%) is easier to chew, highly palatable, and useful for puppies who are reluctant eaters or transitioning from mother's milk. It can also help with hydration. The trade-off is higher cost per calorie, shorter shelf life once opened, and less convenience compared to dry food.

Raw and Fresh Puppy Food: Is It Worth It?

Raw and fresh puppy food can be nutritionally excellent, but it requires much more care than commercial complete foods to ensure it is balanced correctly for a growing puppy's specific needs. This section looks at both raw (typically frozen minces and bones) and fresh-cooked (gently cooked and delivered chilled or frozen) options.

Raw feeding for puppies

Raw feeding, sometimes called BARF (Biologically Appropriate Raw Food) or PMR (Prey Model Raw), involves feeding uncooked meat, raw meaty bones, and offal in specific ratios. The typical PMR ratio is 80% muscle meat, 10% raw edible bone, 5% liver, and 5% other secreting organ. According to the British Veterinary Association (BVA), raw feeding carries genuine risks including bacterial contamination (Salmonella, Campylobacter, and E. coli) for both the puppy and the humans in the household, as well as nutritional imbalances if not properly formulated.

For puppies specifically, the BVA advises extra caution given their developing immune systems. If you choose to feed raw, use a PFMA-member raw food manufacturer such as Natures Menu or Nutriment, which produce complete raw puppy mixes balanced to FEDIAF guidelines. Do not attempt to create your own raw diet without working with a veterinary nutritionist, particularly for puppies.

Price: complete raw puppy mince from reputable UK brands typically costs £5 to £8 per kg. A 10 kg puppy eating roughly 5 to 6% of its body weight daily would require approximately 500 to 600 g per day, costing £2.50 to £4.80 per day.

Fresh-cooked puppy food

Fresh-cooked subscription services deliver gently cooked, human-grade food formulated by veterinary nutritionists. This format avoids the bacterial risks of raw feeding while offering higher ingredient quality and palatability than most dry or wet commercial foods. Bella & Duke's puppy raw range is one of the more accessible options for owners interested in raw feeding with less preparation effort. Their complete puppy mixes are pre-portioned and include the correct ratios of meat, bone, and offal. See Bella & Duke's puppy range and subscription options.

Is raw or fresh food worth it for puppies?

According to Loyal & Loved, raw and fresh food can be worth the investment if you have the time, budget, and commitment to do it properly, and if you source from a reputable, nutritionally complete supplier. It is not the right choice for every household, particularly those with young children, immunocompromised family members, or limited freezer space. A well-formulated premium kibble from a brand like Barking Heads or AATU will give your puppy everything they need without the additional complexity.

How Much Should You Feed a Puppy?

You should feed a puppy according to its current body weight, expected adult weight, and age, using the feeding guide on your chosen food as a starting point, then adjusting based on your puppy's body condition score. There is no single universal amount because calorie requirements vary enormously between a 2 kg Chihuahua puppy and a 30 kg Labrador puppy of the same age.

General daily feeding guidelines by age

The following figures are approximate and based on FEDIAF 2022 energy requirements for puppies. Always use your specific food's feeding guide as the primary reference, because calorie density varies significantly between products.

Puppy Age Meals Per Day General Guidance
8 to 12 weeks 4 Highest calorie needs relative to body weight; stomach capacity is small
3 to 6 months 3 Growth rate is rapid; monitor body condition weekly
6 to 12 months 2 to 3 Small breeds can move to 2 meals; large breeds continue 3
12 months plus 2 Most medium breeds ready to transition to adult food

How to assess body condition

The World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) body condition score (BCS) uses a 9-point scale. A healthy puppy should score 4 to 5 out of 9: you should be able to feel the ribs easily with gentle pressure but not see them prominently. A waist should be visible from above, and there should be a slight abdominal tuck from the side. If your puppy scores 6 or above, reduce portions by 10% and recheck after two weeks.

Large breed puppies need special attention

As Loyal & Loved explains, overfeeding a large or giant breed puppy is one of the most common and consequential mistakes new owners make. Rapid growth driven by excess calories is a significant risk factor for hip dysplasia, elbow dysplasia, and osteochondrosis, according to a 2006 study in the Journal of Nutrition (Hazewinkel et al.) that remains the basis for current veterinary guidance. Large breed puppies should be kept lean throughout their growth phase, which typically lasts until 18 to 24 months.

When to Transition From Puppy to Adult Food?

You should transition your puppy to adult food when they reach approximately 80% of their expected adult body weight, which varies widely by breed size. Switching too early removes the elevated protein and nutrient levels puppies need; switching too late prolongs excess calcium intake that can cause problems in large breeds.

Transition timing by breed size

  • Toy and small breeds (up to 10 kg adult weight): transition at around 9 to 12 months
  • Medium breeds (10 to 25 kg adult weight): transition at around 12 months
  • Large breeds (25 to 45 kg adult weight): transition at around 18 months
  • Giant breeds (over 45 kg adult weight): transition at around 18 to 24 months

How to transition safely

Sudden food changes are a leading cause of digestive upset in dogs, including vomiting and diarrhoea. The standard veterinary recommendation is a gradual transition over 7 to 10 days, mixing increasing proportions of the new adult food with the existing puppy food. A typical schedule looks like this:

  1. Days 1 to 2: 75% puppy food, 25% adult food
  2. Days 3 to 4: 50% puppy food, 50% adult food
  3. Days 5 to 6: 25% puppy food, 75% adult food
  4. Days 7 to 10: 100% adult food

If your puppy shows any signs of digestive upset such as soft stools, vomiting, or reduced appetite, slow the transition down and consult your vet if symptoms persist for more than 48 hours.

Staying with the same brand

If your puppy is doing well on Barking Heads Puppy Days, for example, transitioning to Barking Heads adult range makes the dietary change easier because the ingredient base remains familiar and the palatability profile is consistent. Many brands design their puppy and adult ranges to complement each other for exactly this reason.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best puppy food recommended by vets in the UK?

Vets in the UK most commonly recommend Royal Canin, Hill's Science Plan, and Purina Pro Plan puppy ranges because these brands conduct extensive feeding trials and have robust nutritional research behind them. However, "vet recommended" does not mean these are the only good options. Premium independent brands such as Barking Heads and AATU meet FEDIAF nutritional standards and use higher-quality named ingredients. The best food for your puppy depends on their breed size, any sensitivities, and your budget. Always ask your vet or a registered veterinary nutritionist if you are unsure.

Is dry or wet food better for puppies?

Neither dry nor wet food is inherently better; both can be nutritionally complete and suitable for puppies. Dry food is more cost-effective, easier to store, and may support dental hygiene to a limited degree. Wet food is more palatable for fussy puppies, higher in moisture, and often easier to chew for very young dogs just starting on solid food. Many owners feed a combination, using wet food as a topper or mixer to encourage appetite while keeping the bulk of the diet as dry kibble.

Can I feed my puppy adult dog food?

You should not feed a puppy adult dog food as their sole diet. Adult dog foods do not contain enough protein, calories, or correctly balanced minerals to support healthy puppy growth. The one exception is food labelled "complete for all life stages," which is formulated to meet both puppy and adult requirements. If you accidentally run out of puppy food, one or two meals of a high-quality adult food will not cause harm, but do not continue for more than a day or two before restocking.

How long should a puppy stay on puppy food?

Most puppies should stay on puppy food until they reach approximately 80% of their expected adult body weight. For small breeds this is typically around 9 to 12 months; for large and giant breeds it can be 18 to 24 months. Switching too early, particularly in large breeds, removes important growth-phase nutrition and can increase the risk of developmental problems. Your vet will be able to advise on the right timing for your specific dog's breed and size.

Is grain-free puppy food safe in the UK?

Grain-free puppy food is not banned in the UK and many grain-free formulas meet full FEDIAF nutritional standards. However, the ongoing FDA investigation in the United States into a potential link between grain-free, high-legume diets and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in dogs means many UK vets currently advise caution, particularly in breeds with elevated cardiac risk. If you prefer grain-free for allergy or intolerance reasons, discuss it with your vet and ensure you choose a brand from a PFMA member manufacturer that conducts regular nutritional testing.

What is a good puppy food for small breeds in the UK?

For small breeds (under 10 kg adult weight), look for a food specifically labelled for small breeds or toy breeds, as these have smaller kibble sizes, higher calorie density, and nutrient profiles suited to faster maturation. Good options in the UK include Barking Heads Puppy Days Tiny Paws, Royal Canin Mini Puppy, and AATU 80/20 Puppy Chicken (which comes in a small-kibble format). Small breeds also need more frequent meals when very young, typically four times a day up to 12 weeks, to avoid hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar).
