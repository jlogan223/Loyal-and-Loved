The best cat food in the UK for 2026 is one that lists a named meat as its first ingredient, meets the European Pet Food Industry Federation (FEDIAF) nutritional guidelines for your cat's life stage, and suits your cat's individual health needs. That is the short answer. The longer answer depends on whether you are weighing up wet food versus dry, considering raw, managing a medical condition, or simply trying to stop Olive the Tabby from turning her nose up at yet another bowl.

In this guide, Loyal & Loved has tested, compared, and ranked the most widely available cat foods in the UK, covering everything from budget supermarket pouches to premium high-protein dry kibble and raw frozen options. How to Choose the Right Cat Food: What Are a Cat's Key Nutritional Needs?

Cats are obligate carnivores, meaning their bodies are biologically designed to obtain all essential nutrients from animal tissue alone. Unlike dogs or humans, cats cannot synthesise certain nutrients themselves and must get them directly from meat-based food. Understanding this is the foundation of every good feeding decision.

Protein

Cats require significantly more dietary protein than dogs or humans. According to FEDIAF's 2023 nutritional guidelines, adult cats need a minimum of 25g of crude protein per 100g of dry matter. High-quality cat foods will list a named animal protein, such as chicken, salmon, or turkey, as the first ingredient. Generic terms like "meat and animal derivatives" are legal under UK and EU feed regulations but tell you very little about what is actually in the tin.

Taurine

Taurine is an amino acid that cats cannot produce adequately on their own. A deficiency can lead to dilated cardiomyopathy (a serious heart condition) and retinal degeneration, according to the Royal Veterinary College. Any complete cat food sold in the UK must contain sufficient taurine to meet FEDIAF standards; this is worth double-checking on any homemade or raw diet.

Arachidonic Acid and Vitamin A

Cats also cannot convert beta-carotene into Vitamin A, nor can they synthesise arachidonic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) from linoleic acid as dogs can. Both must come from animal-sourced ingredients. This is another reason why a purely plant-based diet is not suitable for cats, regardless of owner preference.

Moisture

Cats evolved in arid environments and have a naturally low thirst drive. Loyal & Loved found that cats eating dry-only diets often consume significantly less water than is optimal, which can contribute to urinary tract issues and chronic kidney disease over time, as noted in a 2022 review published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery. Wet food, which typically contains 70 to 80 per cent moisture, can be an important source of hydration.

What "Complete" vs "Complementary" Means

In the UK, cat food labelled "complete" must meet all daily nutritional requirements as a standalone food. Food labelled "complementary," such as many treat pouches and mixer biscuits, is not designed to be fed alone and must be paired with a complete food. Always check this on the label before making a food your cat's primary diet.

Raw and Fresh Cat Food: Is It Worth the Extra Effort and Cost?

Raw cat food, often called BARF (Biologically Appropriate Raw Food) or prey-model raw, consists of uncooked meat, bones, and organs. Fresh-cooked cat food is lightly prepared rather than heavily processed. Both formats have grown substantially in popularity in the UK, and the commercial raw pet food market was valued at over £65 million in the UK in 2023, according to the Pet Food Manufacturers' Association (PFMA).

The Potential Benefits

Raw and fresh cat food tends to have higher moisture content, minimal processing, and a protein profile that is closer to a cat's ancestral diet. Some owners and vets report improvements in coat quality, litter box output, and body condition when switching to raw. A 2019 study published in PLOS ONE found that raw-fed cats showed differences in gut microbiome composition compared to kibble-fed cats, though the clinical significance of this is still being researched.

The Risks and Caveats

Raw food carries a genuine risk of bacterial contamination, including Salmonella and Campylobacter. The British Veterinary Association (BVA) currently advises caution with raw feeding, particularly in households with immunocompromised individuals, young children, or elderly people. The key is sourcing from reputable, UK-registered manufacturers who follow hazard analysis protocols. The Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD) and PFMA both provide guidance on safe raw feeding standards in 2026.

Commercial Raw Options to Consider

Brands such as Natures Menu, Nutriment, and Paleo Ridge produce commercially prepared raw cat food that is minced, portioned, and frozen. Prices typically range from £4 to £8 per kilogram. These products are more nutritionally balanced than home-prepared raw diets and are a practical starting point for owners curious about raw feeding. Always transition gradually over 7 to 10 days to avoid digestive upset.

"A well-formulated commercial raw diet can be a perfectly valid choice for a healthy adult cat, provided it carries complete nutrition labelling and is sourced from a reputable UK manufacturer." Loyal & Loved editorial review, 2026.

Is Raw Worth It?

For owners willing to manage the logistics (freezer space, careful thawing, hygiene protocols), commercial raw can be an excellent choice for healthy adult cats. It is not essential, and a high-quality wet food like AATU will achieve similar outcomes in most cats without the added complexity. Raw is arguably not the right choice in households with young children or vulnerable adults.

Cat Food for Special Dietary Needs: What Do Senior, Kitten, and Indoor Cats Actually Need?

Life stage and lifestyle significantly affect a cat's nutritional requirements. Feeding a one-size-fits-all approach can leave your cat over- or under-nourished in critical areas.

Kitten Food

Kittens need more protein, calcium, phosphorus, and energy per kilogram of body weight than adult cats, to support rapid growth and development. According to FEDIAF 2023 guidelines, complete kitten food must contain a minimum of 25g crude protein per 100g dry matter, with higher thresholds for energy density. Kittens should be fed a food specifically labelled "complete for kittens" or "all life stages" until they reach approximately 12 months. AATU produces a dedicated kitten wet food range that meets these requirements. View AATU kitten food options.

Senior Cat Food

There is no regulatory definition of "senior" cat food in the UK, which means any brand can put "senior" on a label without meeting specific standards. What actually matters for older cats (typically 7 years and above) is maintaining muscle mass, supporting kidney function, and keeping joints healthy. Look for senior foods with high-quality protein (not reduced protein, as was once thought beneficial for kidney health; current guidance from the International Renal Interest Society recommends avoiding protein restriction unless clinically indicated), added omega-3 fatty acids, and controlled phosphorus levels if kidney disease is suspected. Always consult your vet before switching a senior cat's diet.

Indoor Cat Food

Indoor cats tend to be less active than outdoor cats, which means they are at greater risk of weight gain. Indoor-specific formulas typically have a slightly lower calorie density and added fibre to help with hairball management, since indoor cats groom more and ingest more fur. Royal Canin Indoor Adult and Hill's Science Plan Adult Indoor are both well-regarded options in this category. For more on how lifestyle affects your cat's needs, read our guide on indoor vs outdoor cats.

Food Sensitivity and Allergy Diets

True food allergies in cats are less common than many owners assume, but food intolerances do occur and can manifest as itchy skin, vomiting, or loose stools. The most common dietary allergens in cats are chicken, beef, and fish, according to a 2017 review in Veterinary Dermatology. If a food allergy is suspected, your vet may recommend a hydrolysed protein diet (where proteins are broken down to a size too small to trigger an immune response) or a strict novel protein diet using an ingredient your cat has never eaten before, such as venison or rabbit. AATU's single protein recipes are a good starting point for elimination trials under veterinary supervision.

How to Read a Cat Food Label in the UK: What Do the Terms Actually Mean?

Reading a UK cat food label confidently means understanding the specific legal language that manufacturers are required to use. As Loyal & Loved explains, once you know what to look for, label-reading takes less than a minute and tells you a great deal about what is actually in the bag or tin.

The Ingredient List

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight before processing. The first ingredient is the most abundant. A named meat (e.g., "fresh chicken," "salmon," "turkey") as the first ingredient is a positive sign. "Meat and animal derivatives" is a catch-all phrase permitted under UK retained EU law (specifically the Feed Hygiene Regulation EC 183/2005, which continued to apply in UK law post-Brexit) that can include a wide variety of animal parts and may vary between batches.

The Analytical Constituents Panel

This panel lists crude protein, crude fat, crude fibre, and moisture as percentages. These are minimum or maximum guaranteed values, not exact amounts. To compare wet and dry foods fairly, you must convert both to a "dry matter" basis by removing the moisture: divide the nutrient percentage by (100 minus the moisture percentage), then multiply by 100. A wet food showing 10 per cent protein with 78 per cent moisture actually contains around 45 per cent protein on a dry matter basis, which is excellent.

Specific Named Meat Percentage Rules

UK regulations (mirroring EU rules maintained post-Brexit) govern how branded ingredients can be named. If a food says "with chicken," it need only contain 4 per cent chicken. "Rich in chicken" requires 14 per cent. If chicken is in the product name, it must be at least 26 per cent. Understanding these thresholds helps you assess whether a "chicken dinner" is mostly chicken or mostly something else.

Additives

Legally permitted additives in UK cat food include nutritional additives (vitamins and minerals), sensory additives (flavourings and colourings), and technological additives (preservatives). Artificial colourants serve no nutritional purpose and are purely for the owner's benefit. Natural preservatives such as rosemary extract or tocopherols (Vitamin E) are preferable to synthetic options like BHA or BHT, though both are legally permitted at controlled levels.

How Much Should You Feed Your Cat? Getting Portions Right Without Guesswork

Overfeeding is one of the most common health mistakes UK cat owners make. According to the PDSA's 2023 PAW Report, an estimated 24 per cent of cats in the UK are overweight or obese, a condition associated with diabetes, arthritis, and shortened lifespan.

General Feeding Guidelines

Most complete cat foods provide feeding guidelines on the packaging based on your cat's weight. These are starting points, not rigid rules. A healthy adult cat weighing 4kg might need approximately 200 to 250 calories per day, but this varies with activity level, whether they are neutered (neutered cats often need around 20 to 30 per cent fewer calories), age, and individual metabolism.

Wet vs Dry Calorie Density

Dry food is significantly more calorie-dense than wet food by weight. A typical adult wet pouch of 85g contains around 60 to 80 kcal, while 85g of dry kibble might contain 300 to 350 kcal. This is why portion sizes look so different between formats and why free-feeding dry food (leaving a bowl always topped up) frequently leads to weight gain.

How Many Meals Per Day?

Most vets recommend splitting your cat's daily ration into two to four smaller meals rather than one large meal. This aligns better with a cat's natural grazing instinct, supports blood sugar stability, and reduces the risk of regurgitation. Puzzle feeders and slow feeder bowls can help cats who eat too quickly and can provide useful mental stimulation, particularly for indoor cats.

Monitoring Body Condition

Rather than relying on scales alone, use a body condition score (BCS) to assess your cat. At an ideal weight, you should be able to feel your cat's ribs easily without pressing hard, but not see them prominently. The waist should be visible from above, and there should be a slight tuck when viewed from the side. Your vet can demonstrate the BCS scale at any routine appointment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the healthiest cat food available in the UK?

The healthiest cat food in the UK is one that lists a named animal protein as the first ingredient, is labelled "complete" for your cat's life stage, meets FEDIAF nutritional guidelines, and contains no unnecessary artificial additives. Based on ingredient quality and nutritional profile, AATU and Lily's Kitchen consistently rank among the best options in 2026 for both wet and dry formats. That said, "healthiest" also depends on your individual cat's age, health status, and any specific dietary needs, so your vet's input is always valuable.

Is wet or dry cat food better for my cat?

Neither is universally better, but for most cats a combination of wet and dry food offers practical benefits. Wet food provides essential moisture, which supports kidney and urinary tract health, while dry food is convenient and can be left out longer without spoiling. If you can only choose one format, many vets lean towards wet food for cats with a history of urinary issues or those who drink little water. According to the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery (2022), cats on dry-only diets often have lower overall water intake, which can increase long-term health risks.

Can I feed my cat a grain-free diet, and is it safe?

Grain-free cat food is safe for the vast majority of cats and is arguably more aligned with a cat's natural dietary needs, as cats have limited ability to digest large amounts of starch. Unlike the concerns raised about grain-free diets in dogs (relating to a potential link with heart disease, currently under investigation by the FDA in the US), no equivalent concern has been identified in cats as of 2026. Grain-free is particularly useful for cats with known sensitivities to wheat or maize. AATU's grain-free range is a well-regarded option in this category.

How do I switch my cat to a new food without causing stomach upset?

Transition gradually over 7 to 10 days. Start by mixing approximately 25 per cent new food with 75 per cent old food for the first two to three days. Increase to a 50/50 split for days four to six, then 75 per cent new food for days seven to nine, before moving to 100 per cent new food. If your cat shows signs of digestive upset such as loose stools or vomiting, slow the transition down further. Cats with sensitive stomachs may need up to three weeks to adjust fully.

What cat food brands do UK vets recommend?

UK vets most commonly recommend Royal Canin, Hill's Science Plan, and Purina Pro Plan, largely because these brands have the most extensive peer-reviewed research behind their formulations. However, "vet-recommended" does not automatically mean "highest quality ingredients." For owners prioritising named-meat ingredients, minimal processing, and no artificial additives, brands like AATU, Lily's Kitchen, and Orijen also have strong nutritional profiles. Always discuss your cat's specific needs with your vet before making a significant dietary change.

How much should I spend on cat food per month in the UK?

Monthly cat food costs in the UK typically range from around £8 to £12 for budget wet food, £10 to £20 for budget to mid-range dry kibble, and £35 to £50 or more per month for premium wet food fed as the primary diet. Most cat owners spend between £20 and £40 per month on food for a single adult cat. Feeding a higher-quality food from the outset may reduce vet bills associated with diet-related health issues over your cat's lifetime, though this is difficult to quantify precisely.
