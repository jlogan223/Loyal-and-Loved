The best cat food in the UK in 2026 is one that lists a named meat source as its first ingredient, meets FEDIAF (the European Pet Food Industry Federation) nutritional guidelines for your cat's life stage, and sits comfortably within your budget. That is the short answer. The longer one, which this guide covers in full, involves understanding the difference between wet, dry, raw and fresh formats; knowing which brands consistently deliver quality; and learning how to read a UK pet food label so you are never misled by clever marketing again.

Whether you are feeding a demanding senior like Olive the tabby or a bouncy kitten who seems to inhale her food, this guide ranks the best options across every price bracket, from under £1 per day to premium fresh and raw subscriptions. All prices quoted are correct as of early 2026.

How to Choose the Right Cat Food: Key Nutritional Needs

Choosing the right cat food starts with understanding that cats are obligate carnivores, meaning they must obtain certain nutrients exclusively from animal tissue and cannot synthesise them from plant sources. According to the FEDIAF Nutritional Guidelines (2023 edition), adult cats require a minimum of 25g of crude protein per 100g of dry matter, alongside essential nutrients that are simply not negotiable.

The non-negotiable nutrients

  • Taurine: An amino acid cats cannot produce in sufficient quantities themselves. Deficiency causes dilated cardiomyopathy and blindness. It must be present in any complete cat food.
  • Arachidonic acid: An omega-6 fatty acid cats cannot synthesise from linoleic acid, unlike dogs. It must come from animal fat.
  • Vitamin A (retinol): Cats cannot convert beta-carotene from plants into usable vitamin A. They need preformed retinol from liver or fish.
  • Vitamin D3: Cats cannot synthesise adequate vitamin D from sunlight, so dietary sources are essential.
  • Niacin (vitamin B3): Cats have very limited ability to synthesise niacin from tryptophan, so meat-based sources are critical.

Wet vs dry vs raw: a quick overview

Wet food (pouches and tins) typically contains 70 to 80% moisture, which supports urinary tract health. According to a 2011 study published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, cats on wet diets produce significantly more dilute urine, reducing the risk of feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD). Dry food (kibble) is convenient, calorie-dense and better for dental health in some cases, though the evidence on kibble and dental hygiene is more nuanced than many brands suggest. Raw and fresh diets aim to mimic the cat's ancestral prey diet and can be highly digestible, but they carry handling and nutritional completeness risks if not properly formulated.

What to look for on the label

Always look for a named protein source (chicken, salmon, turkey) as the first ingredient rather than vague terms like "meat and animal derivatives." A complete food must state it meets the nutritional requirements for the stated life stage. Complementary foods, which are labelled as such, are not nutritionally complete and must not be fed as a sole diet. Loyal & Loved explains this distinction in more detail in the label-reading section below.

Raw and Fresh Cat Food: Is It Worth It?

Raw and fresh cat food diets can be genuinely beneficial when they are properly formulated to meet FEDIAF nutritional standards, but they are not automatically superior to high-quality wet or dry food simply because they contain uncooked ingredients. The British Veterinary Association (BVA) updated its position statement on raw feeding in 2023, noting that while it recognises some owners choose raw diets, it continues to highlight risks of bacterial contamination (including Salmonella and Campylobacter) and nutritional imbalance in home-prepared raw foods.

See Bella & Duke raw cat food options here. The key advantage over home-prepared raw is that Bella & Duke's recipes are independently tested for nutritional completeness.

Is raw worth it?

If you choose raw, use a commercially prepared, complete raw food from a reputable supplier rather than making your own without professional nutritional input. Handle raw food with the same hygiene standards as raw human meat (separate chopping boards, thorough handwashing, no licking of spoons). If anyone in your household is immunocompromised, the BVA recommends against raw feeding entirely due to bacterial risk. For the majority of healthy cats, a high-quality complete wet food will deliver excellent results without the handling complexity.

Cat Food for Special Dietary Needs: Senior, Kitten and Indoor

Cat food for special dietary needs should be chosen based on verified life stage requirements rather than marketing claims. FEDIAF sets separate minimum nutrient profiles for kittens and adult/senior cats, but there is no separate FEDIAF standard for "senior" cats, which means senior labelling in the UK is largely unregulated.

Kitten food

Kittens require significantly higher protein, fat, calcium, phosphorus and taurine than adult cats to support rapid growth and immune development. According to FEDIAF guidelines, kitten foods must meet "growth" nutrient profiles, which mandate higher minimum levels of most nutrients. Feed kitten-specific complete food from weaning (approximately 4 weeks) until around 12 months, or up to 18 to 24 months for large breeds. AATU's kitten range and Royal Canin Kitten are both reliable choices. Browse AATU kitten food here.

Senior cat food

Senior cats (typically considered 7 years and over by UK vets) often benefit from food that is easier to digest, with high-quality protein to maintain muscle mass and reduced phosphorus to support kidney health. However, as the BVA notes, healthy senior cats without diagnosed conditions may not need a specific senior formula. If your cat has been diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (CKD), the most common condition in older cats according to the Royal Veterinary College's VetCompass programme, ask your vet about prescription renal diets (Hill's k/d, Royal Canin Renal) rather than relying on over-the-counter senior food.

Indoor cat food

Indoor cats are at higher risk of obesity and hairballs due to lower activity levels and increased grooming. Look for foods with slightly reduced calorie density and added fibre sources to aid hairball passage. Royal Canin Indoor, Hill's Science Plan Indoor and AATU's weight management options all address this. For a broader discussion of lifestyle differences, our article on indoor vs outdoor cats covers activity needs in detail.

Food for cats with urinary issues

Cats prone to FLUTD or urinary crystals benefit most from increased moisture intake. Switching from a dry-only diet to wet food is often the single most impactful dietary change. Prescription urinary diets (Hill's c/d, Royal Canin Urinary SO) are formulated to reduce urinary pH and mineral levels; these require veterinary recommendation.

How to Read a Cat Food Label in the UK

Reading a UK cat food label correctly means knowing which parts of the label are legally regulated and which are pure marketing. Pet food in the UK is regulated by the Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD) and the Animal Feed Regulations 2010, which implement EU-derived standards. As Loyal & Loved explains below, several terms on labels have specific legal meanings you should know.

Legally regulated terms

  • "Complete": The food on its own meets all known nutritional requirements for the stated life stage. This is the only type you should use as a sole diet.
  • "Complementary": The food does not meet all nutritional requirements and must be fed alongside other foods.
  • "With chicken" or "rich in chicken": Under UK/EU feed labelling rules, "with" means a minimum of 4% of that ingredient, "rich in" means at least 14%.
  • "Chicken dinner" or "chicken flavour": Flavour claims require only trace amounts detectable by the animal, not a significant proportion of the recipe.
  • "Meat and animal derivatives": This legally permitted term covers any part of a slaughtered warm-blooded land animal, including tissue, organs and rendered material. It is not inherently harmful but is less transparent than named ingredients.

The ingredient list

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight before processing. Because wet food contains a high proportion of water, the first ingredient is almost always a protein or water-containing ingredient. For dry food, the order is more meaningful as it reflects dry weight contribution. A named meat (chicken, turkey, salmon) in the first position in a dry food is a strong positive indicator.

The analytical constituents panel

This panel lists crude protein, crude fat, crude fibre and moisture percentages. For meaningful comparisons between wet and dry foods, you need to convert to dry matter basis. Subtract the moisture percentage from 100 to get the dry matter percentage, then divide each nutrient percentage by the dry matter percentage and multiply by 100. This gives you a like-for-like comparison regardless of format.

How Much Should You Feed Your Cat?

How much to feed your cat depends on their body weight, age, activity level, whether they are neutered, and the caloric density of the food you are using. According to the Royal Veterinary College, approximately 39 to 52% of cats seen in UK veterinary practices are classified as overweight or obese, making overfeeding one of the most common and serious nutritional mistakes UK cat owners make.

General daily feeding guidelines

Cat's Weight Approx. Daily Calories Wet Food Equivalent Dry Food Equivalent
3kg (small adult) 150 to 180 kcal 2 to 3 x 85g pouches 45 to 55g kibble
4kg (average adult) 190 to 230 kcal 3 x 85g pouches 55 to 70g kibble
5kg (larger adult) 230 to 280 kcal 3 to 4 x 85g pouches 65 to 80g kibble
Kitten (0.5 to 1kg) 80 to 120 kcal 1 to 2 x 85g pouches 25 to 40g kitten kibble

These are approximate guidelines only. Always use the feeding guide on the specific food you are using as a starting point, then adjust based on your cat's body condition score (BCS). A healthy cat at BCS 4 to 5 out of 9 should have ribs that are easily felt but not visibly prominent, a visible waist when viewed from above, and a small abdominal tuck when viewed from the side. Your vet nurse can show you how to assess this at a free weight check appointment, which most UK vet practices offer.

Should you free-feed or use set meals?

Most UK vets and animal nutritionists recommend portion-controlled set meals (two to three times daily for adults) over free feeding, particularly for indoor cats. Free feeding dry food is one of the most common contributors to feline obesity. If you find it difficult to be consistent, an automatic feeder can help. Puzzle feeders and food-dispensing toys are also excellent ways to slow eating and provide mental enrichment, especially for indoor cats.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the healthiest cat food you can buy in the UK?

The healthiest cat food in the UK is one that is labelled complete for your cat's life stage, lists a named animal protein as its first ingredient, and has a high meat or fish content with minimal artificial additives. In 2026, standout options include AATU (wet and dry), KatKin fresh food, and Purina Pro Plan, depending on your budget and your cat's individual needs. There is no single best food for every cat; health conditions, age and lifestyle all influence the right choice.

Is wet or dry food better for cats?

Both can form a healthy diet, but wet food has a clear advantage for hydration. Cats have a naturally low thirst drive, evolved from desert ancestors who obtained most moisture from prey. A wet-food or mixed wet-and-dry diet supports urinary tract health and is generally recommended by UK vets, particularly for cats prone to kidney disease or urinary crystals. Dry food is more calorie-dense and convenient, but should always be accompanied by fresh water.

How do I switch my cat to a new food without causing an upset stomach?

Transition gradually over 7 to 10 days. Start with approximately 25% new food mixed with 75% old food for the first two to three days, then move to 50/50, then 75% new, then 100%. Cats have sensitive digestive systems and sudden dietary changes can cause vomiting, diarrhoea or food refusal. If your cat refuses the new food entirely, consult your vet; some cats benefit from appetite stimulants or a more gradual approach under professional supervision.

Is grain-free cat food better for cats in the UK?

Grain-free cat food is not automatically better for cats, but it can be beneficial for cats with confirmed grain sensitivities or intolerances. Cats have limited amylase activity (the enzyme used to digest starch), so high-carbohydrate, grain-heavy foods are less biologically appropriate than meat-focused diets. However, the US FDA investigated a possible link between grain-free diets and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in dogs; no equivalent regulatory concern has been raised by the VMD for cats in the UK as of 2026. Choose grain-free foods from established brands with FEDIAF-complete formulations.

How much should I spend on cat food per month in the UK?

A reasonable budget for a healthy adult cat in the UK in 2026 ranges from around £8 to £12 per month for budget complete wet food, £15 to £25 per month for mid-range options such as AATU or James Wellbeloved, and £45 to £75 per month for premium fresh or raw diets such as KatKin or Bella & Duke. Feeding costs should be viewed alongside other ownership costs; ensuring you also have appropriate cover is sensible, so do check our guide to the best cat insurance UK if you have not already.

Can I feed my cat dog food in an emergency?

In a genuine one-off emergency, a single meal of complete adult dog food is unlikely to cause lasting harm. However, dog food must never be used regularly or as a substitute for cat food. Dog foods do not contain sufficient taurine, arachidonic acid or vitamin A for cats, and chronic feeding of dog food to cats can cause serious, life-threatening deficiencies. Always revert to appropriate cat food as quickly as possible.
