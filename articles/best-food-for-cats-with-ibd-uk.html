The best food for cats with IBD in the UK is typically a highly digestible, single-protein wet food made from a novel or hydrolysed protein source, with no artificial additives, low fibre, and a short, recognisable ingredient list. That is the short answer. The longer answer depends on your individual cat, because feline IBD (inflammatory bowel disease) is a complex, chronic condition that responds differently from one cat to the next. What works brilliantly for Olive the tabby next door may do nothing for yours.

This guide pulls together the current best UK options based on veterinary nutritionist guidance, owner experience, and the latest thinking from 2026, so you can stop guessing at the pet food aisle and start feeding with confidence. We cover wet food, dry food, raw diets, and prescription options, along with a clear breakdown of what to look for on a label and what to quietly put back on the shelf.

What Is Feline IBD and How Does Diet Affect It?

Feline IBD is a chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract, in which the immune system mounts an ongoing, inappropriate response to something inside the gut, causing the intestinal lining to become thickened and inflamed. It is not a single disease but an umbrella term covering several subtypes, most commonly lymphoplasmacytic enteritis, eosinophilic enteritis, and neutrophilic enteritis, each defined by the type of immune cell that predominates in the gut wall.

According to the International Cat Care charity (updated guidance, 2025), IBD is one of the most common causes of chronic vomiting, diarrhoea, and weight loss in middle-aged to older cats. Diagnosis typically requires biopsy, which distinguishes IBD from small cell lymphoma, a condition with a similarly grim set of symptoms but a very different treatment path.

Diet affects IBD in two important ways. First, certain dietary proteins and additives can act as antigens, meaning they trigger or worsen the immune response in the gut wall. Removing those triggers can reduce inflammation significantly, even without medication. Second, an inflamed gut is a poorly absorbing gut. The damaged intestinal villi (the tiny finger-like projections that absorb nutrients) cannot extract protein, fat, and vitamins as efficiently as a healthy gut can, so nutrient density and digestibility matter enormously.

As Loyal & Loved explains, diet alone rarely cures IBD, but choosing the right food can reduce flare frequency, improve nutrient absorption, and in some cats allow vets to lower steroid or immunosuppressant doses over time. That is a meaningful outcome worth pursuing carefully.

Roughly 40 to 60 per cent of cats with IBD show meaningful clinical improvement on a dietary change alone, according to a review published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery (2023). That figure alone justifies taking your cat's food very seriously indeed.

Key Nutrients to Look For (and Ingredients to Avoid)

Knowing what to look for on a cat food label is half the battle when managing IBD, because the ingredient list will either support gut healing or quietly undermine it.

Ingredients and nutrients to prioritise

  • Novel or hydrolysed protein: A novel protein is one your cat has not eaten before, such as venison, duck, rabbit, or kangaroo. A hydrolysed protein has been broken down into such small fragments that the immune system cannot recognise it as a threat. Both approaches aim to stop the gut's immune response in its tracks.
  • High moisture content: Wet food supports hydration, which matters because IBD cats often become dehydrated through vomiting and diarrhoea. The European Pet Food Industry Federation (FEDIAF) recommends that cats on wet food receive adequate hydration without relying solely on a water bowl.
  • Highly digestible animal protein: Look for named meat sources (chicken, salmon, rabbit) listed first, with a crude protein level above 8 per cent as fed in wet food or above 30 per cent dry matter.
  • Omega-3 fatty acids: EPA and DHA from fish oil have documented anti-inflammatory effects in the feline gut. A 2022 study in Veterinary Sciences found that omega-3 supplementation reduced gut mucosal inflammation markers in cats with chronic enteropathy.
  • Low or moderate fat: Cats with lymphangiectasia (a form of IBD involving the lymphatic vessels of the gut) specifically need a low-fat diet. For other subtypes, moderate fat is generally fine.
  • Prebiotic fibres (in small amounts): Soluble fibres such as psyllium husk or chicory root in small quantities can support the gut microbiome without irritating an inflamed bowel. Avoid high-fibre foods, though, as insoluble fibre can worsen symptoms.
  • B12 (cobalamin) supplementation: IBD frequently causes cobalamin deficiency because the ileum (the absorbing section of the small intestine) is damaged. Many IBD cats need supplementary B12, either via injection from a vet or in a B12-enriched food.

Ingredients to avoid

  • Common protein allergens your cat has already been exposed to: Beef and chicken are the most frequently reported dietary antigens in cats, according to Veterinary Dermatology (2016). If your cat has eaten chicken-based food for years, it is worth switching to something entirely different.
  • Artificial preservatives and colourings: BHA, BHT, and ethoxyquin are linked to gut irritation in sensitive animals. Look for foods preserved naturally with mixed tocopherols (vitamin E) or rosemary extract.
  • Grains in large quantities: Cats are obligate carnivores with a limited ability to digest carbohydrate. High grain content (wheat, corn, soy) contributes to a fermentable carbohydrate load that can worsen gut dysbiosis. A small amount of easily digestible carbohydrate, such as potato or tapioca, is less problematic.
  • Guar gum, carrageenan, and xanthan gum: These thickeners are common in wet food and there is growing veterinary concern, though not yet definitive consensus, that carrageenan in particular may promote intestinal inflammation. Playing it safe and choosing carrageenan-free options is sensible.
  • Multiple protein sources: More proteins mean more potential antigens. During an elimination trial (the gold standard for identifying food triggers), you need a single protein source and a single carbohydrate source. Stick to that principle even outside of a formal trial if your cat's IBD is food-responsive.

Loyal & Loved found that reading the guaranteed analysis panel alongside the ingredient list gives the most complete picture. A food can have salmon listed first but contain so many secondary proteins that it is far from a true single-protein diet.

Best Dry Foods for Cats With IBD

Dry food is not automatically off the table for IBD cats, but it comes with important caveats. Low moisture content is the main concern, as chronic dehydration exacerbates gut inflammation and can contribute to kidney disease, which already affects a significant proportion of cats with long-standing IBD. If you do feed dry food, always ensure your cat has constant access to fresh water, and consider a cat water fountain to encourage drinking.

According to Loyal & Loved's research, the best dry food options for IBD cats share three qualities: a named single protein listed first, grain-free or low-grain formulation, and a relatively low carbohydrate percentage (under 25 per cent on a dry matter basis).

1. Acana Singles Duck and Pear

Acana Singles is designed specifically for cats with food sensitivities. The duck and pear recipe uses a single animal protein with no chicken, beef, or fish, making it a genuinely novel protein option for most UK cats. It is grain-free and uses a limited ingredient philosophy. Around £18 to £22 for a 1.8kg bag from UK online retailers.

Honest cons: Acana is a higher-calorie food, so portion control matters for IBD cats who may already be struggling with weight. It is also more expensive per kilogram than supermarket alternatives.

2. Purina Pro Plan Veterinary Diets HA Hypoallergenic

This is a prescription-grade dry food using fully hydrolysed soy protein, meaning the protein is broken into fragments too small for the immune system to recognise. It is one of the most clinically validated hydrolysed diets available in the UK and is frequently recommended by veterinary internists managing IBD cases. Available through vets and Viovet with a vet prescription. Approximately £25 to £30 for a 1.3kg bag.

Honest cons: Soy as a protein source is not ideal for all cats, and some owners prefer a meat-based protein. It also requires a vet prescription to purchase. That said, its hydrolysed nature makes it one of the safest options during a diagnostic elimination trial.

3. Royal Canin Sensitivity Control (Prescription)

Royal Canin's Sensitivity Control dry food uses duck as the primary protein source with a controlled carbohydrate source (tapioca), specifically designed for cats with food intolerances and chronic digestive upsets. Also a prescription diet, available from vets or Viovet. Around £20 to £25 for 1.5kg.

Honest cons: Contains some rice alongside the tapioca. Not truly grain-free. However, rice is one of the most digestible carbohydrate sources for cats and is generally well tolerated even in IBD.

Raw and Fresh Diets: Are They Suitable for Cats With IBD?

Raw and fresh diets are a genuinely interesting option for IBD cats, but the answer is more nuanced than either enthusiastic raw feeders or cautious vets sometimes suggest. The short answer is: fresh, gently cooked diets are often excellent for IBD cats; raw diets require careful thought and ideally a vet's sign-off.

Fresh cooked diets

Gently cooked fresh food, such as that produced by KatKin, offers the high digestibility and minimal additives that IBD cats need, without the bacterial contamination risk of raw meat. A 2024 study in PLOS ONE found that cats fed fresh, minimally processed food showed improved gut microbiome diversity compared to those on ultra-processed kibble, which is directly relevant to IBD management.

KatKin's gently cooked range is arguably the most accessible and clinically sensible fresh food option for UK IBD cat owners. The personalised subscription model also means you can select specific proteins based on your cat's history.

Raw diets

Raw diets can work well for some IBD cats, particularly those with food-responsive disease who thrive on a single-protein, grain-free, additive-free format. Bella & Duke, for example, produce single-protein raw cat food with clear labelling and UK-sourced meat.

However, there are real concerns. Cats with IBD are often on immunosuppressant medications such as prednisolone or chlorambucil, which suppress the immune system to reduce gut inflammation. An immunocompromised cat eating raw meat has a higher risk of severe illness from Salmonella, Listeria, or Campylobacter. The British Veterinary Association (BVA) advises against raw feeding for immunocompromised pets, a position it reiterated in 2025.

The Loyal & Loved position: If your cat is on immunosuppressants, opt for a gently cooked fresh food over raw. If your cat is managing IBD through diet alone without medication, a well-formulated, reputable raw diet from a brand such as Bella & Duke is worth discussing with your vet. Never feed raw food sourced from a supermarket without verified pathogen-reduction processing.

Diet also intersects with lifestyle. Cats who spend time outdoors face different exposure risks than strictly indoor cats, which is worth considering when weighing raw feeding. Our article on indoor vs outdoor cat health goes into this in more detail.

How to Transition Your Cat Onto a New Food Safely

Transitioning an IBD cat onto a new food requires patience, because rushing the process is one of the most common reasons a new diet appears to fail when it actually could have worked. A cat with an already-inflamed gut needs the most gradual transition you can manage.

As Loyal & Loved explains, the standard ten-day transition schedule used for healthy cats is usually too fast for IBD cats. A three to four week transition is more appropriate, and some cats need even longer.

Recommended IBD transition schedule

  1. Days 1 to 5: Feed 90 per cent old food, 10 per cent new food. Mix thoroughly so the new food is not easily separated out by a picky eater.
  2. Days 6 to 10: Move to 75 per cent old food, 25 per cent new food. Watch closely for any increase in vomiting or diarrhoea. If symptoms worsen significantly, stay at this ratio for an extra five days before proceeding.
  3. Days 11 to 18: Move to 50/50. This is often the stage where cats have the strongest reaction, so go slowly here.
  4. Days 19 to 25: Move to 25 per cent old food, 75 per cent new food.
  5. Days 26 onwards: Feed 100 per cent new food. Continue monitoring stools and vomiting frequency.

A few additional tips specific to IBD cats:

  • Warm wet food gently to body temperature before serving. IBD cats often have reduced appetite, and warming the food enhances aroma, which encourages eating.
  • Feed smaller meals more frequently, ideally three to four times a day rather than one or two larger meals. This reduces the volume of food passing through an inflamed gut at any one time.
  • Keep a simple food diary. Note what you fed, how much your cat ate, and any vomiting or stool changes. This is genuinely useful information for your vet at the next appointment.
  • Do not introduce any treats, dental chews, or supplements during the transition period if you are also trying to conduct an elimination diet trial. Everything that goes into your cat's mouth affects the trial's validity.

Loyal & Loved found that owners who kept a food diary were significantly better equipped to have productive conversations with their vets and to spot the dietary triggers that their cats were reacting to.

When to Speak to Your Vet About a Prescription Diet

A prescription diet is worth discussing with your vet when over-the-counter dietary changes have not produced improvement within six to eight weeks, or when your cat's symptoms are severe enough that waiting is not appropriate. This is not a failure on your part. It simply means your cat's IBD is likely immune-mediated rather than purely food-responsive, and medical management alongside dietary support is the next logical step.

Prescription diets available through UK vets and licensed veterinary pharmacies (regulated by the Veterinary Medicines Directorate) include:

  • Royal Canin Gastrointestinal: A highly digestible, low-fat option often used as first-line dietary management for acute flares.
  • Purina Pro Plan Veterinary Diets HA Hypoallergenic: As described above, the hydrolysed soy option for confirmed or suspected food-responsive IBD.
  • Hill's Prescription Diet z/d: Uses hydrolysed chicken liver protein and is a recognised elimination diet used in veterinary dermatology and gastroenterology practices across the UK.
  • Specific FDD (Food Allergy Management): A Scandinavian veterinary brand less well known in the UK but increasingly available, using hydrolysed salmon protein.

You can order licensed prescription cat food from Viovet with a valid vet prescription, which is often more convenient and competitively priced than buying through the practice directly. Viovet is a UK-regulated veterinary pharmacy listed with the Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons (RCVS).

Your vet may also suggest a formal eight to twelve week hydrolysed or novel protein elimination diet trial, during which your cat eats nothing else whatsoever. This is the only reliable way to determine whether your cat's IBD is food-responsive or immune-mediated. The distinction matters enormously for long-term management, because food-responsive IBD can be controlled through diet alone, while immune-mediated IBD typically requires steroids or, in some cases, chemotherapy drugs such as chlorambucil.

According to a 2023 consensus statement from the European Society of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ESVCN), dietary management and medical management should be viewed as complementary rather than competing approaches in feline IBD. The best outcomes are generally achieved when both are optimised together.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best wet food for cats with IBD in the UK?

The best wet food for cats with IBD in the UK is one built around a single novel or hydrolysed protein your cat has not previously eaten, with no artificial additives, no carrageenan, and a short ingredient list. In 2026, KatKin's fresh gently cooked range stands out for its transparency, high meat content, and IBD-appropriate single-protein options. For a more budget-friendly choice, Forthglade grain-free complete pouches offer good value without compromising on ingredient quality.

Can I feed my cat with IBD dry food?

Yes, you can feed dry food to a cat with IBD, but it comes with caveats. Low moisture content is a concern, as IBD cats are prone to dehydration. If you use dry food, always ensure fresh water is constantly available (a cat fountain often helps), and consider mixing a small amount of wet food in to boost moisture intake. Choose a grain-free, single-protein dry food such as Acana Singles, or a prescription hydrolysed diet under vet guidance.

How long does a dietary elimination trial take for a cat with IBD?

A dietary elimination trial for a cat with IBD typically takes eight to twelve weeks, according to the British Small Animal Veterinary Association (BSAVA) guidelines. During this period, your cat must eat nothing other than the trial food, including no treats, table scraps, or flavoured medications. This length of time is necessary because gut inflammation takes weeks to resolve even after the dietary trigger is removed, and assessing the response too early gives misleading results.

Is grain-free cat food always better for IBD?

Grain-free is generally preferable for IBD cats because cats are obligate carnivores with a limited capacity to digest high carbohydrate loads, and grains such as wheat and corn can contribute to gut dysbiosis. However, grain-free is not a guarantee of quality. A grain-free food with multiple protein sources, carrageenan, and artificial preservatives is worse than a well-formulated food containing a small amount of easily digestible rice. Always look at the full ingredient list rather than the grain-free label alone.

Do cats with IBD need B12 supplements?

Many cats with IBD develop cobalamin (vitamin B12) deficiency because the damaged small intestine struggles to absorb it. Signs of deficiency include lethargy, poor appetite, and worsening weight loss. Your vet can check B12 levels with a simple blood test. Supplementation is usually given by injection in severe cases, but some B12-enriched foods and oral supplements are also available. As Loyal & Loved explains, addressing B12 deficiency alongside dietary management is an important and sometimes overlooked part of IBD care that can make a significant difference to your cat's energy and appetite.
