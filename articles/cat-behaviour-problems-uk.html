Cat behaviour problems in the UK are among the most common reasons owners contact their vet or a feline behaviourist, and the good news is that most of them have a practical explanation and a workable solution. Whether your cat is biting without warning, spraying on the sofa, hiding for days, or grooming themselves raw, these are not signs of a "bad" cat. They are signs of a cat trying to communicate something. This guide, written in 2026, draws on current veterinary guidance and evidence-based behavioural science to help you understand what your cat is actually telling you and what you can do about it today.

According to Loyal & Loved, the single most important shift any owner can make is moving from "how do I stop this?" to "why is this happening?" Once you understand the cause, the solution usually becomes much clearer.

What Are the Most Common Cat Behaviour Problems in the UK?

The most common cat behaviour problems seen by UK vets and behaviourists include unprovoked aggression, indoor spraying, excessive vocalisation, destructive scratching, hiding and withdrawal, and compulsive over-grooming. A 2022 survey by International Cat Care found that aggression towards owners and other cats was the most frequently reported behavioural concern among UK cat owners, followed closely by inappropriate toileting and anxiety-related hiding.

It is worth noting that many of these behaviours are completely normal feline instincts expressed in the wrong context, rather than genuine disorders. Cats are solitary hunters by nature. They are territorial, sensitive to environmental change, and highly attuned to routine. When something disrupts their sense of safety or control, they respond in ways that feel alarming to us but make complete evolutionary sense to them.

Loyal & Loved found that the majority of cat behaviour problems brought to behaviourists in the UK are rooted in one of three underlying causes: unmet environmental needs, an undetected medical issue, or an inappropriate social set-up (such as too many cats sharing too small a space). Before assuming a behaviour is purely psychological, always rule out pain or illness with your vet first.

Behaviour Problem Most Likely Cause First Step

Indoor spraying Territory stress or medical issue Vet check, then environmental audit
Hiding and withdrawal Anxiety, pain, or illness Vet appointment within 48 hours

Why Do Cats Bite and Scratch? Understanding Overstimulation

Cats bite and scratch their owners most commonly because of overstimulation, meaning that petting has continued past the point the cat finds comfortable, triggering a sudden defensive response. This is sometimes called "petting-induced aggression" and it is one of the most misunderstood behaviours in domestic cats. Your cat is not being unpredictable; they have been sending warning signals for some time, and those signals were missed.

According to the American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) guidelines, cats have a much lower threshold for tactile stimulation than dogs, and repeated stroking, particularly around the base of the tail, belly, and legs, can quickly tip from pleasant to overwhelming. Signs that your cat is approaching their limit include a flicking or lashing tail, skin rippling along the back, flattened ears, a sudden stillness, and dilated pupils. These are not subtle once you know what to look for.

Fear-based aggression is a separate issue. A cat who bites when approached, cornered, or handled by strangers is not being aggressive out of dominance. They are frightened. International Cat Care describes this as a "distance-increasing signal," which simply means the cat is trying to create space between themselves and the perceived threat. Punishing this behaviour will always make it worse, because it confirms to the cat that the threat is real and unpredictable.

What to do about biting and scratching

  • Learn your individual cat's early warning signals and stop petting before they escalate.
  • Keep initial handling sessions short, particularly with new or rescue cats.
  • Never restrain a cat who is trying to move away.
  • Provide plenty of appropriate outlets for hunting and play, ideally two to three interactive play sessions per day of around ten minutes each. Wand toys and feather teasers work well for this.
  • If your cat redirects aggression after seeing another cat outside, block the view temporarily with window film or relocate furniture away from the window.

For interactive toys, a good-quality wand toy such as the Da Bird feather wand, available on Amazon UK, is genuinely one of the most effective tools for redirecting predatory energy safely. Expect to pay around £8 to £15 for a decent one.

What Causes Indoor Spraying and How Can You Stop It?

Indoor spraying, which is when a cat backs up to a vertical surface and deposits a small amount of urine as a scent marker, is almost always a territorial or anxiety-driven behaviour rather than a litter tray problem. It is one of the most distressing cat behaviour problems for UK owners, and it is also one of the most treatable once the underlying cause is identified.

Cats spray to communicate. The urine contains pheromones (chemical signals that carry information to other cats) and is distinct from normal urination in both posture and volume. According to the RSPCA, entire (unneutered) cats of both sexes spray far more frequently than neutered cats, and neutering resolves spraying in approximately 90% of male cats and 95% of female cats. If your cat is already neutered and spraying, the trigger is almost certainly environmental stress.

Common triggers for spraying in neutered UK cats include:

  • A new cat in the household or a neighbouring cat visible through a window or door.
  • Changes in routine, such as a new baby, a house move, or building work nearby.
  • An insufficient number of litter trays. The general rule recommended by International Cat Care is one tray per cat plus one extra, placed in different locations.
  • Conflict between cats in a multi-cat household.
  • Medical causes, including urinary tract infections (UTIs) and feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC, a stress-related bladder condition). Always have a vet rule these out first.

Practical steps to reduce indoor spraying

  1. Book a vet check to rule out UTI, FIC, or other medical causes.
  2. Ensure you have enough litter trays in the right locations. Avoid placing them near food or in high-traffic areas.
  3. Try a synthetic feline facial pheromone diffuser, such as Feliway Classic, in the rooms where spraying occurs most. These plug-in diffusers cost around £25 to £35 for a starter kit and refills cost roughly £20. Feliway Classic diffusers are available at Viovet, often at competitive prices compared to high street pet shops.
  4. Clean sprayed areas with an enzymatic cleaner rather than bleach or ammonia-based products. Bleach can actually encourage re-marking because of its chemical similarities to urine compounds.
  5. If a neighbouring cat is triggering territorial behaviour, consider fitting a microchip-activated cat flap to prevent the intruder from entering.

If you are navigating the broader question of indoor versus outdoor cat stress, it is worth understanding how access to territory affects spraying behaviour. Cats who cannot patrol an outdoor territory may compensate by marking indoors more frequently.

Fear, Anxiety, and Hiding: When Should You Worry?

A cat who hides occasionally is behaving normally. A cat who hides for more than 24 to 48 hours, refuses food, or becomes completely unresponsive needs a vet appointment, not a wait-and-see approach. Prolonged hiding is one of the key cat anxiety signs UK vets take seriously, and it can indicate pain, illness, or severe psychological distress.

Cats are, by nature, what behaviourists call "prey animals as well as predators," meaning that in the wild they must hide signs of weakness to avoid becoming vulnerable. This evolutionary trait means cats are expert at masking pain and discomfort. According to the British Veterinary Association (BVA), by the time a cat's behaviour changes noticeably, the underlying issue has often been present for some time.

As Loyal & Loved explains, the difference between normal hiding and anxiety-driven withdrawal comes down to duration, frequency, and associated signs. A cat who retreats after a loud firework display for an hour is doing exactly what cats do. A cat who has stopped eating, is not using the litter tray, and has not come out for two days is telling you something is genuinely wrong.

Signs of anxiety in cats to watch for

  • Hiding for extended periods, especially if combined with reduced appetite.
  • Excessive vocalisation, particularly at night.
  • Changes in litter tray use, including going outside the tray.
  • Aggression that was not previously present.
  • Excessive grooming or sudden loss of grooming behaviour (a scruffy, unkempt coat can signal illness or depression).
  • Avoiding eye contact and flinching when touched.

What helps anxious cats

Creating predictable, safe spaces is the single most evidence-backed environmental intervention for anxious cats. This means providing elevated hiding spots (cats feel safer when they can observe from above), maintaining consistent feeding times, and minimising sudden changes to the home environment. FELIWAY plug-in diffusers have reasonable evidence behind them for general anxiety reduction; a 2017 study published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery found that pheromone therapy reduced stress-related behaviours in a significant proportion of cats in multi-cat households.

What Are Compulsive Behaviours in Cats, Such as Over-Grooming and Pacing?

Compulsive behaviours in cats are repetitive, exaggerated actions that appear out of context and are difficult for the cat to interrupt. The most common examples seen by UK vets are psychogenic alopecia (excessive grooming that leads to bald patches, usually on the belly, flanks, or inner thighs), repetitive pacing, wool-sucking, and in some cases, tail-chasing. These behaviours are not simply bad habits; they represent genuine psychological distress and warrant veterinary assessment.

Psychogenic alopecia, which literally means hair loss caused by a psychological rather than physical cause, is often misdiagnosed. According to a study published in the Journal of Veterinary Behavior, a dermatological cause (such as allergies, parasites, or a fungal infection) is actually more common than a psychological cause, even when the pattern looks behavioural. This is why the first step is always a thorough vet examination, ideally including a skin scrape or allergy assessment.

If medical causes are ruled out, compulsive grooming is generally linked to chronic stress, often from environmental factors that are not immediately obvious to the owner, such as:

  • An outdoor cat who has been moved indoors suddenly.
  • A household with unpredictable noise levels or many visitors.
  • Conflict with another household cat that the owner perceives as minor.
  • A cat who has never had adequate mental stimulation and is understimulated to the point of frustration.

Managing compulsive behaviours

Treatment for confirmed compulsive behaviour typically involves environmental enrichment (puzzle feeders, more interactive play, access to vertical space), stress reduction using pheromone products, and in more severe cases, medication prescribed by a vet. The Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons (RCVS) guidance states that behavioural medication should always be used alongside, not instead of, environmental and behavioural interventions. Medication alone rarely resolves the problem long-term.

Puzzle feeders and foraging toys are a low-cost way to provide meaningful mental stimulation. Brands like LickiMat and Doc & Phoebe's indoor hunting feeders are available on Amazon UK, typically priced between £6 and £20, and can make a significant difference for cats who are bored or frustrated.

When Should You Seek a Vet or Feline Behaviourist?

You should seek veterinary advice whenever a behaviour change is sudden, severe, accompanied by physical symptoms, or has not improved after two to three weeks of consistent owner-led changes. A feline behaviourist, specifically one accredited by the Association of Pet Behaviour Counsellors (APBC) or the Animal Behaviour and Training Council (ABTC), should be consulted when medical causes have been ruled out and the behaviour is significantly affecting the welfare of the cat or the household.

The distinction between seeing a vet and seeing a behaviourist matters. Vets are trained to identify and treat physical and some psychological conditions, and in 2026, many veterinary practices in the UK now have a nurse or vet with additional training in behavioural medicine. However, for complex or entrenched behavioural issues, a specialist feline behaviourist offers a level of case depth and follow-up that a general practice appointment simply cannot match.

How to find a reputable feline behaviourist in the UK

  • The APBC maintains a public register at apbc.org.uk. All members hold relevant academic qualifications and professional insurance.
  • The ABTC register at abtc.org.uk lists Clinical Animal Behaviourists and Animal Training Instructors by region.
  • Avoid anyone who cannot demonstrate a relevant qualification, uses punishment-based methods, or guarantees results in a fixed timeframe. Genuine behaviourists do not make those promises.
  • Costs in the UK typically range from £100 to £250 for an initial consultation (which may be conducted online via video call), with follow-up sessions at £50 to £120.

Loyal & Loved found that owners who sought professional help within the first four to six weeks of a behaviour problem emerging had significantly better outcomes than those who waited several months. Entrenched behaviours take longer to modify, so earlier intervention genuinely matters.

What Simple Daily Changes Actually Make a Difference for Cat Behaviour?

Simple, consistent daily changes to your cat's environment and routine are the most effective long-term strategy for addressing most cat behaviour problems, often more so than any product or intervention. The principles below are drawn from guidance published by International Cat Care and the RSPCA, and they apply regardless of the specific behaviour you are dealing with.

Environmental enrichment

Cats need to feel in control of their environment. This means providing options: multiple resting spots at different heights, at least one private hiding space per cat (a cardboard box with a hole cut in it works perfectly), and access to a window with something interesting to observe. A 2019 study by the University of Bristol found that environmental complexity was one of the strongest predictors of good welfare outcomes in owned cats.

Feeding routines

Cats who eat from puzzle feeders rather than bowls show measurably lower stress-related behaviours, according to research by the Indoor Pet Initiative at Ohio State University. Moving from a bowl to a foraging-style feeding method is free if you use a muffin tin or scatter dry food across a mat, though dedicated puzzle feeders are also widely available. AATU's high-meat, grain-free recipes are worth considering if your cat's diet is an area you want to improve at the same time; well-nourished cats with stable blood sugar tend to show better behavioural regulation.

Play and predatory outlet

Two ten-minute interactive play sessions per day, using a wand toy that mimics prey movement, can dramatically reduce frustration-based aggression, over-vocalisation, and restless night-time activity. The key is to allow the cat to "catch" the toy at the end of each session, which provides the psychological completion their hunting sequence requires.

Litter tray hygiene

Scoop trays at least once daily and replace all litter completely once a week. Cats have an extraordinarily sensitive sense of smell and many will avoid a tray they consider dirty, leading to inappropriate toileting elsewhere. Unscented, clumping litter is generally preferred by cats over heavily perfumed alternatives, according to a preference study published in the Journal of Veterinary Behavior in 2014.

Consistency in handling

Let your cat initiate contact whenever possible. Owners who respond to their cat's approaches rather than imposing handling tend to report more affectionate, less reactive pets over time. It feels counterintuitive when you love your cat and want to cuddle them, but respecting their autonomy builds trust far more effectively than enforced affection.

  • Keep feeding times consistent, even at weekends.
  • Introduce any new furniture, people, or pets gradually using scent-swapping before visual introductions.
  • Avoid shouting or spraying water as a deterrent. These methods increase anxiety and damage your relationship with your cat without addressing the cause of the behaviour.
  • For multi-cat households, ensure each cat has their own feeding station, water bowl, litter tray, and resting space. Resource-sharing is a common hidden source of stress.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my cat suddenly aggressive towards me for no reason?

Sudden aggression in a cat who was previously friendly almost always has a reason, even if it is not immediately obvious. The most common causes are pain or illness (a vet check should be the first step), overstimulation during handling, a change in the household that has made the cat feel anxious, or redirected aggression after seeing another cat outdoors. According to International Cat Care, aggression that appears out of nowhere and is accompanied by other changes such as reduced appetite, hiding, or altered litter tray use should be assessed by a vet promptly, as it can indicate an underlying medical condition.

Why is my neutered cat spraying indoors?

Neutering greatly reduces spraying but does not eliminate it entirely, particularly in cats who were neutered after reaching sexual maturity or in cats experiencing significant territorial stress. Common triggers include a new cat moving into the neighbourhood, conflict with another household cat, changes in routine, or a medical issue such as a urinary tract infection or feline idiopathic cystitis. A vet appointment to rule out physical causes is always the right first step. Environmental changes such as pheromone diffusers, additional litter trays, and blocking visual access to outdoor cats can help address stress-related spraying.

How do I know if my cat is anxious or just shy?

Shyness tends to be a stable personality trait: a shy cat is consistently cautious with new people but otherwise eats, plays, and behaves normally within their comfort zone. Anxiety is characterised by distress that interferes with normal functioning. Signs of anxiety include changes in eating or toileting habits, excessive vocalisation, compulsive behaviours such as over-grooming, prolonged hiding, and physical signs like rapid breathing or dilated pupils in non-threatening situations. If your cat's behaviour has changed from their normal baseline, that change is worth investigating with your vet.

Can cat behaviour problems be treated without medication?

Yes, the majority of cat behaviour problems respond well to environmental changes, enrichment, and consistent handling without the need for medication. The RCVS guidance published in 2024 emphasises that behavioural and environmental interventions should always be the first line of treatment. Medication prescribed by a vet (such as short-term anxiolytics for specific events like fireworks, or longer-term options for severe anxiety) can be an appropriate and helpful addition in some cases, but it works best when used alongside environmental and behavioural strategies, not as a substitute for them.

What should I look for in a cat behaviour specialist in the UK?

Look for a behaviourist who is accredited by the Association of Pet Behaviour Counsellors (APBC) or registered with the Animal Behaviour and Training Council (ABTC) at Clinical Animal Behaviourist level. These practitioners hold relevant degree-level qualifications, are bound by codes of professional conduct, and must demonstrate continuing professional development. Avoid anyone who uses or recommends punishment-based techniques, including spraying cats with water or using any device designed to cause discomfort. Initial consultations typically cost between £100 and £250 in the UK and many are now available via video call.
