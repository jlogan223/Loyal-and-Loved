Keeping pets cool in a UK heatwave means providing constant access to fresh water, shaded rest areas, and avoiding exercise during the hottest part of the day, typically between 11am and 3pm. Heat stroke in dogs can develop within minutes in high temperatures, and cats, though more heat-tolerant by nature, are far from immune. This guide pulls together the latest 2026 vet guidance and practical, tested advice to help you keep your dog or cat safe when British summers push into dangerous territory.

According to Loyal & Loved, the UK's recent summers have brought extended periods above 30°C, conditions that are genuinely outside what most British pet owners have ever had to plan for. The advice in this article reflects that reality, not the old assumption that a warm day in Britain is nothing to worry about.

Understanding Heat Stroke Risk in Dogs and Cats: Why Is It So Dangerous?

Heat stroke, defined as a dangerous rise in core body temperature above 39.2°C in dogs and cats (known clinically as hyperthermia), occurs when an animal cannot dissipate heat fast enough to keep its body at a safe temperature. Unlike humans, dogs cool themselves almost entirely through panting, not sweating. Cats also pant under extreme stress, but their primary cooling mechanism is grooming: they spread saliva on their fur to create an evaporative effect. Both methods become overwhelmed very quickly in high ambient temperatures.

According to a 2016 study published in Scientific Reports by the Royal Veterinary College (RVC), one in seven dogs with heat stroke in the UK died as a result. The RVC's VetCompass programme, which continues to monitor heat-related illness in 2025 and 2026, identifies exercise-induced heat stroke as the most common cause, accounting for roughly 74% of cases. The remaining cases are largely confinement-related, such as being left in a car or a greenhouse-like conservatory.

Cats are at lower risk overall because they instinctively seek shade and rest, but senior cats, overweight cats, and those with heart or respiratory conditions can deteriorate rapidly in high heat. As Loyal & Loved explains, the danger for cats often goes unnoticed precisely because they hide when they feel unwell, which can delay treatment.

Signs Your Pet Is Overheating: What Symptoms Should You Look For?

The earliest signs of overheating in pets are easy to miss, but recognising them quickly can be the difference between a close call and a tragedy. Below are the key warning signs for dogs and cats.

Signs of overheating in dogs

  • Excessive, heavy panting that does not settle with rest
  • Drooling more than usual, sometimes with thick or foamy saliva
  • Bright red or very pale gums (healthy gums should be bubble-gum pink)
  • Lethargy, confusion, or appearing "drunk" on their feet
  • Vomiting or diarrhoea, sometimes with blood
  • Collapse or seizures in severe cases

Signs of overheating in cats

  • Panting (this is not normal in cats and should always be taken seriously)
  • Restlessness or distress, followed by sudden lethargy
  • Drooling or wet fur around the mouth from grooming attempts
  • Hot to the touch, particularly on the ears and paw pads
  • Vomiting or stumbling

Loyal & Loved found that many owners mistake early heat stress for ordinary tiredness after a walk. If your dog or cat is showing more than one of the above signs, act immediately: move them to a cool area, offer water, and begin gentle cooling with cool (not cold) water. Then call your vet.

Hydration Strategies During Heatwaves: How Do You Keep Pets Properly Hydrated?

Proper hydration is the single most effective preventative measure against heat-related illness in pets. A dog needs roughly 50ml of water per kilogram of body weight per day under normal conditions; that requirement rises significantly in hot weather and after exercise.

Here are practical strategies that work in a UK heatwave:

  • Multiple water stations: Place bowls in several rooms and in any outdoor space your pet uses. Animals in discomfort often will not bother to walk far for water.
  • Ice cubes: Adding a few ice cubes to the water bowl keeps it cooler for longer and encourages many dogs to drink more. Olive the tabby finds them deeply suspicious, but most dogs consider them a bonus treat.
  • Pet water fountains: Cats in particular are more likely to drink from moving water. A pet fountain encourages intake and keeps water oxygenated and fresh. Expect to spend between £20 and £60 for a decent model; you can browse pet water fountains on Amazon.
  • Wet food: Switching to wet food during a heatwave, or adding water to dry kibble, meaningfully increases your pet's overall fluid intake. Brands like AATU offer high-quality wet food with a short ingredient list and good moisture content.
  • Frozen treats: For dogs, freezing low-sodium chicken broth or plain natural yoghurt in an ice cube tray or a lick mat provides hydration, cooling, and enrichment in one. Never use xylitol-containing products.

One point worth making clearly: do not restrict water access in the mistaken belief that a very thirsty dog will drink too fast and bloat. Allowing free access to water is always the right call in hot weather.

Creating Cool Spaces Indoors and Outdoors: Where Should Your Pet Rest in the Heat?

Creating a genuinely cool environment for your pet is about more than cracking a window. In a British home without air conditioning, which applies to the vast majority of UK households, you need to be strategic.

Indoors

  • Close curtains and blinds on south-facing windows during peak sun hours. This can reduce indoor temperature by several degrees without any equipment at all.
  • Use fans effectively: A fan alone moves warm air around. Position a bowl of iced water or a frozen water bottle in front of the fan to create a simple evaporative cooler.
  • Cooling mats: These gel-filled pads activate on contact with your pet's body weight and can stay cool for several hours. They require no electricity or refrigeration. Models from Arf Pets or The Green Pet Shop are widely available for between £15 and £45 depending on size; check cooling mat prices on Amazon.
  • Tiled floors: If you have a kitchen or bathroom with stone or ceramic tiles, let your pet rest there. Luna the Golden Retriever is famously devoted to the kitchen floor on any day above 22°C.

Outdoors

  • Paddling pools: A shallow paddling pool in the shade is one of the most effective cooling tools for dogs. Omlet's outdoor pet ranges and various Amazon listings offer purpose-built dog pools from around £20 upwards. Browse Omlet's outdoor products or find a dog paddling pool on Amazon.
  • Shade structures: Never leave a pet in direct sun with no means of escape. A garden parasol, a tarpaulin canopy, or a dedicated shade sail will do the job. Check that the shaded area does not trap humidity.
  • Damp towels: Laying a cool, damp towel on the ground for your pet to lie on is an old-fashioned trick that genuinely works. Replace it every hour or so as it warms up.

Safe Exercise and Walking Times in Hot Weather: When Is It Safe to Walk Your Dog?

The safest times to walk your dog during a UK heatwave are before 8am and after 7pm, when tarmac temperatures have dropped to safer levels. The "seven-second rule" is a reliable practical test: press the back of your hand firmly onto the pavement for seven seconds. If it is too hot to hold there comfortably, it is too hot for your dog's paw pads.

Tarmac can reach 60°C on a 30°C day in direct sunlight, according to research cited by the Dogs Trust. At that temperature, paw pad burns can occur within 60 seconds of contact.

Practical adjustments for summer walks:

  • Shorten walks significantly. A 20-minute sniff-focused walk in the cool of the morning beats a 45-minute route in midday heat.
  • Choose grassy routes, parks, or woodland paths over pavements where possible.
  • Carry water and a collapsible bowl for any walk over 10 minutes.
  • Let your dog set the pace. If they want to stop, let them stop.
  • For senior dogs in heat, reduce exercise even more drastically; old joints and ageing hearts cope poorly with the dual demands of exertion and high temperature.

Cats generally self-regulate their outdoor activity, but if yours is an indoor-outdoor cat, consider keeping them indoors during the hottest part of the day and ensuring they can always access a cool indoor space.

Grooming and Coat Care During Summer Heat: Should You Shave Your Dog?

The question of whether to clip or shave a dog in summer is one of the most common, and most misunderstood, areas of pet care in hot weather. The short answer, supported by veterinary dermatologists, is: it depends entirely on the coat type, and shaving a double-coated breed is almost always a mistake.

A dog's double coat (present in breeds such as Golden Retrievers, Huskies, and Bernese Mountain Dogs like our own Bear) acts as insulation in both directions: it traps cool air close to the skin and blocks radiant heat from above. Shaving this coat removes that protection and can cause permanent coat damage, a condition known as post-clipping alopecia.

What you should do instead:

  • Brush regularly: Removing dead undercoat with a suitable deshedding tool (such as a Furminator-style brush, available for £15 to £40 on Amazon) dramatically improves airflow through the coat.
  • Trim, do not shave: For single-coated dogs or those with long furnishings (like Mabel the Cockapoo), a summer trim by a professional groomer can help. A tidy-up at a UK groomer typically costs between £35 and £70 depending on breed and size.
  • Keep the coat clean: A dirty, matted coat traps heat. A gentle summer bath followed by thorough drying keeps the coat functioning as it should.
  • Check for matts: Matted fur creates pockets of trapped heat and moisture, which can cause skin infections in hot weather. Barking Heads offer coat-friendly shampoos suited to sensitive summer skin; browse Barking Heads grooming products.

For cats, regular brushing to remove excess fur is usually sufficient. Most cats handle their own grooming effectively, but longhaired breeds such as Persians or Maine Coons may benefit from a professional groom in summer.

Never Leave Pets in Hot Cars: Why Is It So Deadly?

Leaving a dog or cat in a parked car on a warm day is one of the fastest routes to a fatal heat stroke. On a 22°C day, the temperature inside a car can reach 47°C within an hour, even with a window left slightly open, according to the RSPCA.

The legal position in the UK as of 2026 is clear: while there is no single specific offence of leaving a pet in a hot car, it can constitute an offence under the Animal Welfare Act 2006, which requires owners to meet their animal's needs and protect them from suffering. The RSPCA and police have powers to break into vehicles to rescue animals in distress, and prosecutions do follow.

"Not long" is never long enough. A dog can begin to show signs of heat stroke within six minutes in a hot vehicle. There is no errand that justifies that risk. If you are travelling and must stop, always take your pet with you, or travel at times when another person can remain with the animal in a running, air-conditioned car.

If you see a dog in a hot car showing signs of distress, the RSPCA advises calling 999 immediately. The police have the power to act; the RSPCA does not hold legal powers of entry in this situation without police assistance.

When to Contact Your Vet in an Emergency: How Do You Know If It Is Serious?

Contact your vet immediately, without waiting, if your pet shows any of the following: collapse, seizures, bloody vomiting or diarrhoea, unresponsiveness, or panting so severe that they cannot settle at all. These are signs of advanced heat stroke and represent a genuine emergency.

While you are making the call or travelling to the practice, begin first aid:

  1. Move your pet to a cool, shaded, or air-conditioned space immediately.
  2. Offer small sips of cool (not ice cold) water if they are conscious and can swallow.
  3. Apply cool, wet towels to the neck, armpits, and groin, which are areas where blood vessels are close to the surface. Replace them frequently as they warm up.
  4. Use a fan to increase evaporative cooling if available.
  5. Do not use ice packs directly on the skin. The shock of extreme cold can cause blood vessels to constrict, trapping heat in the body's core.
  6. Transport to the vet as quickly and calmly as possible, with the car air conditioning running.

Be aware that emergency vet care for heat stroke can be significant in cost, often running to several hundred pounds for hospitalisation, IV fluids, and monitoring. This is one of many reasons why pet insurance heat-related claims are worth understanding before a crisis occurs.

Breed-Specific Heat Risks: Which Dogs and Cats Are Most Vulnerable?

Not all pets face equal risk in the heat. Certain breeds are physiologically more susceptible to heat stroke, and knowing whether your pet falls into a high-risk category should shape how proactively you act.

Flat-faced (brachycephalic) dogs

Brachycephalic breeds, a term meaning "short-skulled," include French Bulldogs, Pugs, Bulldogs, Shih Tzus, and Cavalier King Charles Spaniels. The compressed anatomy of their airways means they cannot move air efficiently through panting. According to the Royal Veterinary College's 2024 brachycephalic health report, French Bulldogs are over 14 times more likely to suffer heat stroke than Labrador Retrievers. These dogs should be treated as high-risk from any temperature above 20°C.

Large and heavy-coated breeds

Large dogs such as Bernese Mountain Dogs (like our Bear), Saint Bernards, and Newfoundlands generate significant metabolic heat during exercise. Their size means a greater volume of heat to dissipate. Keep walks very short, prioritise water access, and watch them closely for early signs of distress.

Senior, overweight, and unwell dogs

Dogs over the age of eight, dogs carrying excess weight, and those with heart disease, breathing conditions, or laryngeal paralysis are all at heightened risk. For these animals, extra caution is not optional. See our guide on senior dogs in heat for detailed, age-specific advice.

Vulnerable cat breeds and individuals

Persian cats, Scottish Folds, and British Shorthairs face similar brachycephalic challenges to flat-faced dogs. Elderly cats, particularly those with kidney disease (a very common condition in senior cats), are also vulnerable because compromised kidneys make it harder to manage fluid balance. Any cat on regular medication should be monitored especially carefully in high heat.

The Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD) notes that some medications can affect a pet's ability to regulate temperature; if your pet is on any long-term prescription, it is worth a quick call to your vet at the start of a heatwave to ask whether any additional precautions are advised.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my dog has heat stroke?

The most recognisable signs of heat stroke in a dog are heavy, relentless panting that does not settle with rest, bright red or very pale gums, excessive drooling, confusion or staggering, and in serious cases, collapse or vomiting. If your dog is showing more than one of these signs after being in the heat, treat it as an emergency: begin cooling with cool water and contact your vet immediately. Do not wait to see if they improve on their own.

What temperature is too hot to walk a dog in the UK?

As a general guideline, walking a dog in temperatures above 20°C carries some risk, particularly for flat-faced breeds, large dogs, elderly dogs, and those who are overweight. Above 25°C, the risk becomes significant for most breeds, and above 28°C, most dogs should not be exercised outdoors during daylight hours at all. Use the seven-second pavement test: if you cannot hold the back of your hand on the tarmac for seven full seconds, it is too hot for your dog's paws. Stick to early morning and late evening walks during heatwaves.

Can cats get heat stroke?

Yes, cats can get heat stroke, though they are somewhat more heat-tolerant than dogs because they instinctively seek shade and rest. However, flat-faced cats (such as Persians and British Shorthairs), elderly cats, overweight cats, and those with underlying health conditions are genuinely at risk. If a cat is panting, stumbling, drooling, or appears very lethargic in hot weather, this is a medical emergency. Contact your vet straight away.

Should I shave my dog in summer to keep them cool?

For most dogs, and particularly those with double coats such as Golden Retrievers, Huskies, Border Collies, and Bernese Mountain Dogs, shaving is not recommended and can actually make them hotter by removing the insulating layer that helps block radiant heat. It can also cause permanent coat damage. Instead, brush your dog regularly to remove dead undercoat, which improves airflow, and keep the coat clean and matt-free. For single-coated or long-furnished dogs, a professional trim (not a shave) can help; ask your groomer for advice specific to your dog's coat type.

Is it illegal to leave a dog in a hot car in the UK?

There is no single specific law in the UK that names hot cars explicitly, but leaving a dog in a hot car that causes suffering is prosecutable under the Animal Welfare Act 2006. In practice, police and the RSPCA do intervene and prosecutions do occur. As of 2026, animal welfare organisations continue to campaign for clearer legislation. Regardless of the precise legal position, the practical reality is that a dog can begin to suffer heat stroke within six minutes in a hot car. The safest rule is simply never to leave your pet in a parked vehicle in warm weather.
