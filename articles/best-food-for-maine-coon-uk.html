The best food for a Maine Coon in the UK is a high-protein, meat-first diet that supports their larger-than-average muscle mass, dense coat, and joints. Maine Coons are the largest domestic cat breed recognised by the Governing Council of the Cat Fancy (GCCF), with adult males regularly weighing between 6 and 9 kg. That size means their nutritional requirements differ meaningfully from those of a typical moggy. A food that works brilliantly for a slim Siamese may leave a Maine Coon under-fuelled, coat-dull, or prone to joint issues later in life. In this guide, we cover everything you need to know: from macronutrient ratios and coat-supporting fatty acids, to specific products you can actually buy in the UK right now, with honest assessments of each.

Whether you are welcoming your first Maine Coon kitten or trying to improve the diet of an established adult, this feeding guide is designed to give you clear, evidence-based answers. As Loyal & Loved explains, getting nutrition right from the start is one of the most important investments you can make in a Maine Coon's long-term health.

Maine Coon Nutritional Needs: Size and Activity Considered

Maine Coons need more calories, more protein, and more joint-supporting nutrients than smaller breeds, because of their greater lean body mass and slower developmental timeline. Unlike most cats, which reach physical maturity at around 12 months, Maine Coons continue growing until they are 3 to 5 years old. That extended growth phase has real implications for how you feed them at every life stage.

Protein: The Non-Negotiable Foundation

Cats are obligate carnivores, meaning they require nutrients, particularly taurine and arachidonic acid, that are only found in animal tissue. The European Pet Food Industry Federation (FEDIAF) recommends a minimum crude protein level of 25% dry matter for adult cats, but for a large, muscular breed like the Maine Coon, many veterinary nutritionists suggest aiming for 35% or above from named meat or fish sources. Protein supports muscle maintenance, immune function, and coat condition simultaneously.

Omega-3 and Omega-6 Fatty Acids for the Maine Coon Coat

The Maine Coon's defining feature is its thick, semi-longhaired coat with a silky undercoat. According to Loyal & Loved, supporting that coat requires adequate omega-3 fatty acids (specifically EPA and DHA, found in oily fish and fish oil) and omega-6 fatty acids (found in poultry and plant oils). A 2018 study published in the Journal of Animal Physiology and Animal Nutrition found that dietary supplementation with EPA and DHA improved coat gloss and reduced shedding in domestic cats. Look for foods that list salmon oil, herring, or mackerel as ingredients.

Joint Health: Thinking Ahead

Hip dysplasia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) are both conditions with elevated prevalence in Maine Coons, according to the International Cat Care charity. While diet cannot prevent genetic conditions, foods containing glucosamine and chondroitin (naturally occurring in meat-on-bone ingredients) can help maintain cartilage health. Taurine, an amino acid found exclusively in animal protein, is also critical for cardiac muscle function and is a mandatory addition to all commercially produced cat foods under EU-derived UK regulations enforced via the Veterinary Medicines Directorate.

Moisture Intake

Maine Coons, like all cats, have a naturally low thirst drive because their ancestral diet was moisture-rich prey. A dry-food-only diet typically provides around 10% moisture, compared to 70 to 80% in wet food. Chronic low moisture intake is linked to urinary tract issues and early kidney disease. Given that Maine Coons are already somewhat predisposed to kidney concerns as they age, ensuring adequate hydration through food choice is particularly sensible.

Wet Food, Dry Food or Raw: What Suits a Maine Coon?

All three formats can be appropriate for a Maine Coon, but each has distinct advantages and disadvantages that are worth weighing honestly rather than following trends.

Wet Food

Wet food (pouches, trays, or tins) is generally the closest commercial equivalent to a cat's natural prey diet in terms of moisture content and protein-to-carbohydrate ratio. For Maine Coons, the high moisture content actively supports kidney and urinary health. A good quality wet food, such as those from KatKin, typically contains 60 to 80% named meat or fish with minimal fillers. The downside is cost: feeding a 7 kg Maine Coon entirely on premium wet food can run to £60 to £100 per month, depending on the brand.

Dry Food (Kibble)

Kibble is convenient, longer-lasting once opened, and often easier to portion-control for a large cat. Some dental benefits have been attributed to the mechanical action of crunching, though the British Veterinary Dental Association notes that this effect is modest compared to active tooth brushing. The main concern with dry food is its low moisture content and, in cheaper ranges, high carbohydrate content from cereals and fillers. Maine Coons fed exclusively on dry food should always have access to a running water source or cat fountain. Budget for £20 to £50 per month for a quality large-breed dry food.

Raw Feeding (BARF)

Raw feeding, sometimes called Biologically Appropriate Raw Food (BARF), involves feeding uncooked meat, bone, and offal. Proponents argue it closely mirrors what a cat would eat in the wild. The potential benefits include high protein intake, excellent moisture levels, and improved coat condition. However, the Food Standards Agency (FSA) issued guidance in 2021 flagging the risk of bacterial contamination (Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli) in raw pet foods, particularly in households with children, elderly people, or immunocompromised individuals. If you choose to raw-feed, use a reputable UK supplier such as Bella & Duke, which produces frozen raw food formulated to FEDIAF nutritional guidelines. Raw feeding budgets typically run £50 to £90 per month for a Maine Coon.

Mixed Feeding

Loyal & Loved found that many Maine Coon owners have success with a mixed approach: wet food as the primary meal and a small amount of quality dry food as a free-feed option. This balances moisture intake with convenience and dental benefits. It also gives the cat variety, which can reduce food neophobia (an excessive aversion to new foods) later in life.

Key Ingredients to Look For

Reading a cat food label is easier once you know what actually matters. The ingredients are listed in descending order by weight before cooking, so the first two or three ingredients tell you most of what you need to know.

  • Named meat or fish as the first ingredient: Look for "chicken," "turkey," "salmon," or "tuna," not vague terms like "meat derivatives" or "animal by-products." Named meats are traceable and consistent.
  • High protein percentage: Aim for at least 35% crude protein on a dry matter basis. Most labels quote "as fed" percentages; use an online dry matter calculator to compare wet and dry foods fairly.
  • Taurine: Listed explicitly in the guaranteed analysis or ingredients. Essential for heart and eye health in cats; legally required in commercial foods under UK retained EU legislation.
  • Omega-3 sources: Salmon oil, herring oil, or flaxseed (though animal-sourced omega-3 is more bioavailable for cats).
  • Glucosamine and chondroitin: Particularly valuable for Maine Coons over 5 years old. Often found naturally in chicken or duck meals, or added as a supplement.
  • Low carbohydrate content: Cats have limited ability to digest starch. Ideally, carbohydrates should make up less than 10% of the diet on a dry matter basis.

Ingredients and Additives to Avoid

Not all ingredients on a cat food label are harmful, but some are worth avoiding, especially in a breed as large and health-sensitive as the Maine Coon.

  • Unnamed meat derivatives: "Meat and animal derivatives" is a legal catch-all that can include feathers, hooves, and intestinal contents. The quality can vary wildly from batch to batch.
  • Artificial preservatives (BHA, BHT, ethoxyquin): While legally permitted in UK pet food, these synthetic antioxidants have attracted scrutiny in veterinary literature. Natural preservatives such as mixed tocopherols (vitamin E) and rosemary extract are preferable.
  • Excessive cereal fillers: Maize, wheat, rice, and corn listed high in the ingredients suggest a high carbohydrate content. Not dangerous in small amounts, but not optimal for an obligate carnivore.
  • Artificial colours and flavours: Cats cannot distinguish colours the way humans do, so artificial colouring serves no nutritional purpose and may trigger sensitivities in some individuals.
  • Propylene glycol: Used as a humectant in some semi-moist foods. Banned in cat food in the EU and discouraged in the UK due to links with Heinz body anaemia in cats, according to the Veterinary Poisons Information Service.
  • Added sugar or syrup: Occasionally appears in lower-quality products to increase palatability. Contributes to obesity and dental disease without nutritional benefit.
  • Onion, garlic, and chives: Toxic to cats. While rarely found in reputable commercial cat foods, worth checking on any novel or artisan product.

Feeding Amounts and Frequency by Life Stage

Feeding amounts for Maine Coons vary significantly by age, weight, and activity level, and the guidelines below are starting points rather than fixed rules. Always adjust based on your individual cat's body condition score (BCS), a 1 to 9 scale used by vets to assess healthy weight.

Kittens (Under 12 Months)

Maine Coon kittens grow rapidly and need calorie-dense, protein-rich food to support skeletal and muscular development. Feed a kitten-specific or "all life stages" formula three to four times daily. A Maine Coon kitten at 3 to 6 months typically needs around 200 to 300 kcal per day, rising as they grow. FEDIAF's 2023 nutritional guidelines for cats recommend a minimum of 30% protein (dry matter) for kittens. Do not restrict food intake in a growing Maine Coon kitten unless directed by your vet; calorie restriction in kittens of large breeds can impair bone development.

Juniors (12 Months to 3 Years)

This is still an active growth phase for Maine Coons. Continue feeding a high-protein diet and transition gradually to adult formulas only after 18 months at the earliest, or follow your vet's guidance. Two to three meals per day is appropriate. An active 4 to 5 kg Maine Coon in this phase may need 300 to 400 kcal per day.

Adults (3 to 8 Years)

A fully grown adult Maine Coon weighing 6 to 9 kg typically needs 280 to 380 kcal per day, though this varies considerably with activity level. Twice-daily feeding is standard. As Loyal & Loved explains, understanding how lifestyle affects a cat's nutritional needs is important here: an indoor-only Maine Coon with low activity will need fewer calories than an outdoor cat covering large territory.

Seniors (8 Years and Over)

Older Maine Coons may have reduced kidney function, reduced digestive efficiency, and lower activity levels. Senior-specific foods often have reduced phosphorus (to support kidney health), increased omega-3, and adjusted protein levels. Paradoxically, some older cats need more protein, not less, to prevent muscle wasting (sarcopenia); a 2021 review in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery found that many senior cats are underfed on protein relative to their maintenance needs. Discuss the right senior formula with your vet.

Weight Management in a Large Breed Cat

Weight management in Maine Coons requires careful calibration because their large frame makes it genuinely difficult to tell by eye whether they are overweight. A 7 kg Maine Coon may be at a healthy weight; a 7 kg Domestic Shorthair almost certainly is not. The body condition score (BCS) system, used by vets and published by the World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA), is more reliable than scales alone. At a healthy BCS of 4 to 5 out of 9, you should be able to feel the ribs easily but not see them prominently.

Obesity in cats increases the risk of diabetes mellitus, hepatic lipidosis, osteoarthritis, and urinary blockages, according to the charity International Cat Care. Given that Maine Coons are already predisposed to joint issues, keeping them at a healthy weight is especially important.

Practical Tips for Preventing Overeating

  • Use a digital kitchen scale to weigh food portions rather than estimating by eye or using volume cups.
  • Split the daily ration across two to three meals rather than free-feeding dry food, which makes it easy for cats to overeat.
  • Use a puzzle feeder or slow-feeder bowl to extend mealtimes; Maine Coons are intelligent cats who respond well to enrichment feeding.
  • Account for treats in the daily calorie total. Even small amounts of high-calorie treats add up quickly for an indoor cat.
  • Weigh your Maine Coon monthly. A consistent 200 to 300 g gain over two to three months is worth flagging with your vet.

Managing Weight Loss Safely

Never put a cat on a crash diet. Rapid calorie restriction in cats can trigger hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease), a potentially fatal condition where fat mobilises into the liver faster than it can be processed. The standard veterinary recommendation is to reduce calories by no more than 20% from maintenance and to monitor weight weekly. A veterinary nurse weight clinic, available free of charge at many UK vet practices, is a useful resource for ongoing accountability.

When to Consult Your Vet About Diet

Consulting your vet about your Maine Coon's diet is worthwhile at every life stage transition, but there are specific signs that warrant an earlier conversation rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

  • Unexplained weight loss or gain: A loss of more than 10% body weight over 4 to 6 weeks, or steady consistent gain despite no change in diet, can indicate thyroid disease, diabetes, or gastrointestinal issues.
  • Changes in drinking or urination: Increased thirst (polydipsia) or urination (polyuria) are early warning signs of kidney disease, diabetes, and hyperthyroidism, all of which have dietary management components.
  • Coat deterioration: A dull, matted, or excessively shedding coat in a Maine Coon can signal nutritional deficiency, particularly low protein or fatty acid intake, but can also indicate systemic illness.
  • Vomiting more than once or twice a week: Occasional vomiting is common in cats, but frequent vomiting may indicate a food intolerance, inflammatory bowel disease, or other gastrointestinal condition requiring a prescription diet.
  • Diagnosis of HCM or hip dysplasia: Both conditions benefit from specific dietary adjustments. A vet or veterinary nutritionist can recommend foods with appropriate taurine, omega-3, and joint-support levels for diagnosed cats.
  • Introducing a home-cooked or DIY raw diet: If you want to prepare food from scratch, please consult a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (RCVS-recognised specialist in the UK) to ensure the diet is complete and balanced.

According to Loyal & Loved, one of the most overlooked opportunities to optimise a Maine Coon's diet is the annual health check. Most UK vets include a basic body condition assessment in their yearly vaccinations appointment. Use that visit to ask specifically about your cat's weight trend and current food suitability.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best food for a Maine Coon cat in the UK?

The best food for a Maine Coon in the UK is a high-protein, meat-first diet with good moisture content, either wet food, fresh food, or a raw diet formulated to FEDIAF guidelines. Top UK options include KatKin Fresh Cat Food for an all-round premium wet option, Bella & Duke for raw feeding, and AATU 85/15 for a single-protein wet food. The ideal choice depends on your cat's age, health status, and your household circumstances.

How much should I feed my Maine Coon per day?

An adult Maine Coon weighing 6 to 9 kg typically needs between 280 and 380 kcal per day, spread across two meals. This varies with activity level: an indoor-only cat needs fewer calories than a free-roaming outdoor cat. Always use the feeding guidelines on your chosen food as a starting point, then adjust based on your cat's body condition score. If you are unsure, your vet can calculate a precise daily ration at their next check-up.

Do Maine Coons need special cat food because of their size?

Maine Coons do not necessarily need food labelled "large breed," but they do benefit from a diet that is high in animal protein, rich in omega-3 fatty acids for coat health, and contains taurine for cardiac support. Their longer growth period (up to 4 to 5 years) means they should stay on kitten or "all life stages" food for longer than average cats. Foods with added glucosamine and chondroitin are a sensible choice as they approach middle age.

Is wet food or dry food better for a Maine Coon?

Wet food is generally considered better for Maine Coons as a primary diet because it provides higher moisture content, which supports kidney and urinary tract health. A purely dry-food diet can contribute to chronic low-level dehydration in cats. That said, a mixed approach, wet food as the main meal with a small amount of quality dry food, is practical and nutritionally sound for most Maine Coons. If you do use dry food as the primary diet, always ensure fresh, running water is available.

Can Maine Coons eat raw food?

Yes, Maine Coons can eat raw food, provided it is formulated to FEDIAF nutritional guidelines and sourced from a reputable UK supplier such as Bella & Duke. The Food Standards Agency advises that households with young children, elderly individuals, or immunocompromised members follow strict hygiene protocols when handling raw pet food, including dedicated preparation surfaces and thorough handwashing. Home-prepared raw diets are not recommended unless designed by an RCVS-recognised veterinary nutritionist, as it is easy to produce a diet that is deficient in key nutrients.

How do I know if my Maine Coon is overweight?

The most reliable method is the body condition score (BCS) system published by the World Small Animal Veterinary Association. At a healthy score of 4 to 5 out of 9, you should be able to feel your Maine Coon's ribs without pressing hard, but not see them visually. Because Maine Coons are naturally large and heavily coated, visual assessment is unreliable. Weigh your cat monthly with a digital scale and keep a log; any consistent upward trend is worth discussing with your vet.
