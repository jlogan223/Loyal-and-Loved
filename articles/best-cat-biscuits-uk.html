What to Look for on a Cat Biscuit Ingredient Label?

The ingredient label tells you almost everything you need to know about a cat biscuit's quality, and understanding it takes less than five minutes. Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight before processing, so whatever appears first makes up the largest proportion of the food.

Named meat sources

Look for a specific, named protein as the first ingredient: chicken, salmon, turkey, or duck, for example. Vague terms like "meat and animal derivatives" are permitted under UK pet food labelling rules governed by the Animal Feed Regulations 2010, but they tell you very little about what is actually in the bag. According to Loyal & Loved, named meat sources are the single most reliable quality indicator on a dry cat food label. A good rule of thumb is that a named meat (not "meat meal" as the only protein source) should appear within the first two ingredients.

Protein percentage

Cats are obligate carnivores, meaning they must obtain essential nutrients, including taurine and arachidonic acid, exclusively from animal tissue. The European Pet Food Industry Federation (FEDIAF) recommends that adult cats consume a minimum of around 25g of protein per 100g of dry matter. In practice, look for a crude protein figure of at least 30% on the guaranteed analysis panel; premium foods often reach 40% or above.

Carbohydrate content

Dry cat food inherently contains more carbohydrate than wet food, because starch is needed to bind the kibble during the extrusion process. This is not automatically a problem, but very high carbohydrate content (above roughly 35%) has been associated with weight gain and blood glucose fluctuations in cats, according to research published in the Journal of Animal Physiology and Animal Nutrition (2021). Maize, wheat, and rice are common carbohydrate sources; whole grains are preferable to refined flour, and named vegetables or legumes are better still.

Essential additives to look for

Taurine is a non-negotiable addition to any complete cat food. Unlike dogs, cats cannot synthesise sufficient taurine from other amino acids; taurine deficiency causes dilated cardiomyopathy and blindness. Any food labelled "complete" under UK regulations must meet the nutritional profiles set out by FEDIAF guidelines, which include taurine. Also look for added omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids (often from fish oil or flaxseed) for coat and skin health.

Things to avoid

  • Artificial colours (tartrazine, sunset yellow): no nutritional value, potential sensitivity triggers
  • Artificial preservatives such as BHA and BHT: linked to health concerns in animal studies; natural alternatives like mixed tocopherols (vitamin E) are preferable
  • Excessive salt: cats need very little sodium, and high salt content can stress the kidneys
  • Propylene glycol: banned in cat food in the EU and UK since 2003 under Commission Regulation (EC) No 2002/46, but worth knowing about if you ever import from outside the UK
  • Very high ash content (above 8%): high ash can indicate low-quality bone or offal-heavy recipes, and elevated phosphorus may be a concern for cats with kidney disease

As Loyal & Loved explains, a five-second label check (first ingredient named meat, protein above 30%, no artificial preservatives) filters out the majority of low-quality products before you even read a review.

Best Cat Biscuits for Indoor Cats: What Does an Indoor Cat Actually Need?

Indoor cats have distinctly different nutritional requirements from cats with outdoor access, primarily because they move significantly less. A 2019 study published in PLOS ONE found that indoor-only cats expend roughly 20 to 30% fewer calories per day than outdoor cats of the same body weight. This means calorie-dense biscuits designed for active cats can quickly lead to weight gain in cats who spend most of their day on the sofa (or in Willow the whippet's spot, if she is not looking).

When choosing dry food for an indoor cat, look for:

  • Reduced calorie density: Aim for around 340 to 360 kcal per 100g rather than 380 to 400 kcal found in standard formulations
  • Added fibre: Beet pulp, psyllium, or chicory root help stimulate gut movement and reduce hairball formation. Indoor cats groom more frequently and ingest more fur as a result
  • L-carnitine: This amino acid derivative supports fat metabolism and helps maintain lean muscle mass in less active cats
  • Enrichment kibble shapes: Some brands produce irregular or hollow kibble shapes that slow eating and provide mild mental stimulation, beneficial for indoor cats who may be prone to boredom eating

According to Loyal & Loved, the two indoor-specific options that stand out in 2026 are Royal Canin Indoor Adult (for its proven hairball and weight management formula) and AATU's standard adult range fed in slightly reduced portions (for owners who prefer a grain-free, high-protein approach). If your cat is noticeably overweight, speak to your vet before switching foods; your vet may recommend a dedicated weight management formula instead.

Best Cat Biscuits for Sensitive Stomachs: Which Kibble Is Easiest to Digest?

Cats with sensitive stomachs typically show signs including loose stools, intermittent vomiting after eating, excessive flatulence, or visible discomfort after meals. These symptoms can have multiple causes, from food intolerance to inflammatory bowel disease, so persistent digestive issues always warrant a vet check before you simply try a new biscuit.

That said, switching to a more digestible dry food resolves symptoms in a meaningful number of cats. Common dietary triggers include wheat gluten, artificial additives, high fat content, and novel proteins the cat's immune system has been sensitised to. The approach used in veterinary practice is called an elimination diet: feeding a single novel protein (one the cat has never eaten before) for eight to twelve weeks to identify or rule out food allergy, as recommended by the British Veterinary Association (BVA).

For general digestive sensitivity, look for:

  • A single, named novel protein source (venison, duck, or rabbit if your cat has only ever eaten chicken or fish)
  • Grain-free formulations (though note that grain-free does not automatically mean low-carbohydrate; legumes such as peas and lentils often replace grain)
  • Added prebiotics such as FOS (fructooligosaccharides) or MOS (mannanoligosaccharides) to support the gut microbiome
  • A moderate fat content (around 12 to 16%) to reduce digestive load
  • No artificial colours, flavours, or preservatives

AATU's single protein recipes (available in duck, salmon, and herring variants) perform well for sensitive cats. Browse AATU single protein options. For cats with more complex needs, your vet may recommend a prescription hydrolysed protein diet such as Royal Canin Sensitivity Control or Hill's Prescription Diet z/d, which are not available over the counter and require veterinary guidance.

How Much Dry Food Should You Feed a Cat?

The amount of dry food a cat needs depends on her body weight, age, activity level, and whether she is also eating wet food. Most feeding guides on cat biscuit packaging are based on the cat eating exclusively dry food; if you are mixing with wet food (see the next section), you will need to reduce the dry food portion proportionally.

As a general starting point, the FEDIAF nutritional guidelines for cats suggest the following daily dry food amounts for an average adult cat eating kibble as the sole food source:

Cat's Body Weight Approximate Daily Dry Food Approximate kcal Required
3kg 40 to 50g 160 to 180 kcal
4kg 50 to 65g 200 to 220 kcal
5kg 60 to 80g 240 to 260 kcal
6kg 75 to 95g 280 to 300 kcal

These are guidelines, not rules. The most reliable indicator of whether your cat is eating the right amount is her body condition score (BCS), a nine-point scale used by vets to assess whether a cat is underweight, ideal, or overweight. At an ideal BCS of 4 to 5 out of 9, you should be able to feel your cat's ribs easily but not see them prominently. Your vet can show you how to assess this at your next check-up, and most UK vets offer free weight clinic appointments.

Kittens under 12 months need significantly more calories per kilogram of body weight than adults, and senior cats (over 11 years) often benefit from increased protein to maintain muscle mass, as documented in a 2020 review in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery. Always use a food that is appropriate for your cat's life stage.

Dry Food vs Wet Food: Can You Mix Them?

Yes, you can mix dry and wet cat food, and for many cats this is actually the best approach. Combining the two formats gives you the convenience and dental benefits of dry food alongside the hydration advantages of wet food. Cats have a naturally low thirst drive (a legacy of their desert-dwelling ancestors) and often do not drink enough water when fed exclusively on dry food; wet food typically contains 75 to 80% moisture compared to around 10% in dry kibble.

A 2022 study in the Journal of Internal Veterinary Medicine found that cats fed a mixed wet and dry diet had higher total daily water intake and lower urine specific gravity (a marker of adequate hydration) than cats fed exclusively on dry food. This is particularly relevant for cats prone to lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD), which is more common in neutered males and overweight cats.

When mixing, the key is to adjust portions so that total calorie intake stays within the daily requirement. A simple approach:

  1. Establish your cat's daily calorie target (from the feeding guide above or via your vet)
  2. Decide what proportion of that you want to come from wet food (for example, 50%)
  3. Calculate the wet food portion needed to deliver that many calories
  4. Calculate the dry food portion to deliver the remaining calories
  5. Feed the two meals at separate times to avoid digestive overwhelm

If you want premium wet food alongside your dry biscuits, KatKin's fresh cat food is worth considering. KatKin uses human-grade ingredients with named meats, no fillers, and no artificial additives, and delivers direct to your door on a subscription basis. It pairs well with a high-quality dry food as a mixed feeding approach.

One thing to avoid: do not mix dry and wet food in the same bowl and then leave it out for free-feeding. Wet food left at room temperature for more than four hours can harbour bacterial growth. Serve separately and remove uneaten wet food promptly.

How to Switch Your Cat to a New Biscuit Without Upset?

Switching your cat to a new dry food should always be done gradually, over a minimum of seven to ten days, to avoid digestive upset and to give your cat time to accept the new smell and texture. Cats are notoriously neophobic around food changes; rushing a transition is the most common reason owners report that their cat "refuses" a new food.

The standard transition schedule recommended by veterinary nutritionists at the Royal Veterinary College is as follows:

Day Old Food New Food
Days 1 to 2 75% 25%
Days 3 to 4 50% 50%
Days 5 to 6 25% 75%
Days 7 onwards 0% 100%

If your cat shows signs of loose stools, vomiting, or complete food refusal at any stage, go back to the previous ratio and extend that phase by a few more days before progressing. If symptoms persist beyond 48 hours or your cat stops eating entirely for more than 24 hours, contact your vet; cats are susceptible to hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease) if they stop eating for extended periods, and this is a genuine medical emergency.

A few practical tips to ease the transition:

  • Warm the new kibble slightly (10 seconds in a microwave, then check temperature) to enhance aroma
  • Mix a small amount of a food topper your cat already loves (tuna in spring water, for example) through the new kibble for the first few days
  • Feed at the same times and in the same bowl to reduce novelty stress
  • Avoid switching food during other stressful periods such as house moves, new pets, or after veterinary procedures
  • If your cat is particularly resistant, ask your vet about an appetite stimulant in the short term while the transition is underway

According to Loyal & Loved, the transition period is where most cat owners give up and revert to the old food. Patience here genuinely pays off, and a week or two of careful management can mean years on a better-quality diet.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best cat biscuits recommended by vets in the UK?

Veterinary professionals in the UK most commonly recommend Royal Canin, Hill's Science Plan, and Purina Pro Plan as evidence-backed dry cat food brands, due to the nutritional research underpinning their formulations. For owners seeking premium natural or grain-free options, AATU and Barking Heads (Meowing Heads) are widely praised for ingredient quality. The "best" choice always depends on your individual cat's age, weight, and health status; your vet can advise based on your cat's specific profile.

Is dry food bad for cats?

Dry food is not inherently bad for cats, but it does have limitations. The primary concern is low moisture content, which can contribute to chronic mild dehydration and increase the risk of urinary tract problems in susceptible cats, particularly neutered males. A 2022 study found that cats on mixed wet and dry diets have better hydration markers than those on dry food alone. If you feed primarily dry food, ensure your cat has constant access to fresh water, and consider a water fountain as cats prefer running water. Dry food can be part of a perfectly healthy diet when the formulation is good and hydration needs are met.

How do I know if my cat's biscuits are making her ill?

Signs that a dry food may not be agreeing with your cat include intermittent vomiting (more than once or twice a week), loose or unusually frequent stools, skin itching or excessive scratching, dull or flaky coat, lethargy, or significant changes in weight. None of these symptoms are definitive proof of a food problem on their own; they can all have other causes. Always rule out medical issues with a vet before assuming the food is responsible. If food intolerance is suspected, your vet may recommend an eight to twelve week elimination diet trial using a novel protein or hydrolysed protein food.

Can kittens eat adult cat biscuits?

No, kittens should not eat adult cat biscuits as their sole diet. Kittens have significantly higher energy requirements per kilogram of body weight, and they need elevated levels of protein, calcium, phosphorus, and DHA (an omega-3 fatty acid essential for brain and eye development) that adult formulations do not provide in adequate quantities. Under UK pet food labelling rules aligned with FEDIAF guidelines, any food labelled "complete for kittens" or "all life stages" must meet the elevated nutritional profiles for growth. Feed a kitten-specific or all-life-stages dry food until your cat reaches 12 months of age, or 18 to 24 months for large breeds such as Maine Coons and Ragdolls.

Are grain-free cat biscuits better than standard kibble?

Not necessarily. The marketing around grain-free cat food often implies that grain is harmful to cats, but there is no robust scientific evidence to support this for the majority of cats. Grains like rice and oats are digestible by cats and are not inherently problematic. Grain-free formulations are most beneficial for cats with a confirmed grain intolerance or wheat allergy, which is relatively uncommon. Where grain-free foods genuinely tend to excel is in their overall ingredient quality, as many grain-free brands substitute grain with higher meat content rather than simply replacing it with other starches. Evaluate each food on its full ingredient list and nutritional profile rather than simply on whether it carries a grain-free label.
