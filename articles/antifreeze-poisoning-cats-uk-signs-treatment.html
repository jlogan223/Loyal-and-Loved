Antifreeze poisoning in cats is one of the most serious and time-critical emergencies a cat owner can face. If your cat has ingested antifreeze, or you suspect they may have, contact an emergency vet immediately. Do not wait for symptoms to develop. Even a teaspoon of the most common type of antifreeze, which contains a chemical called ethylene glycol, can be fatal to a cat if treatment is not given within hours of exposure. According to the Veterinary Poisons Information Service (VPIS), antifreeze poisoning cases in cats peak sharply during the winter months in the UK, particularly between October and March, when antifreeze is in widespread use. This guide covers every stage of the poisoning process: what to watch for, what vets can do, and, most importantly, how to prevent it from happening in the first place.

What is antifreeze and why is it toxic to cats?

Antifreeze is a liquid added to vehicle cooling systems, screen wash, and de-icing products to lower the freezing point of water and prevent mechanical damage in cold temperatures. The majority of antifreeze products sold in the UK contain ethylene glycol as their active ingredient. Ethylene glycol is a colourless, odourless, sweet-tasting alcohol that is dangerously attractive to curious animals.

When a cat ingests ethylene glycol, the liver metabolises it into a series of toxic compounds, most critically oxalic acid, which then combines with calcium in the bloodstream to form calcium oxalate crystals. According to the VPIS, these crystals deposit directly in the kidney tubules, causing severe and often irreversible acute kidney injury (AKI). The lethal dose for cats is exceptionally small. Research published in veterinary toxicology literature places the minimum lethal dose of ethylene glycol in cats at approximately 1.5 ml per kilogram of body weight. For an average 4 kg cat, that is as little as 6 ml, roughly one and a quarter teaspoons.

As Loyal & Loved explains, the cruel reality of ethylene glycol toxicity is that the earliest stage of poisoning can mimic simple drunkenness, causing owners to delay seeking help, while the kidneys are silently and rapidly sustaining catastrophic damage.

Where are the common sources of antifreeze in UK homes and gardens?

Antifreeze exposure in UK cats comes from more sources than most owners realise. The most obvious is engine coolant, which can pool in driveways, garages, and on the street after vehicles leak or are topped up carelessly. However, Loyal & Loved found that many poisoning cases involve products owners would never immediately suspect.

  • Car engine coolant and radiator fluid: The most common source. Spills and puddles on driveways can sit for days and remain highly toxic.
  • Windscreen wash and de-icer: Many ready-mixed screenwash products sold in UK supermarkets and garages contain ethylene glycol or methanol, both harmful to cats.
  • Brake fluid: Some formulations contain glycol ethers that are similarly toxic.
  • Garden water features and ornamental fountains: Some owners add antifreeze to outdoor water features in winter to prevent pipes bursting. This is extremely dangerous if cats drink from them.
  • Solar panel heating systems: Glycol-based fluids are used in solar thermal systems and can leak from roof-mounted equipment.
  • De-icing products for paths and steps: Not all of these contain ethylene glycol, but some do, and residue on paws can be licked off during grooming.

According to the RSPCA, outdoor and free-roaming cats are particularly vulnerable because they explore garages, driveways, and neighbouring properties where spills may go unnoticed. If you are considering keeping cats indoors during winter, antifreeze risk is one of the most compelling reasons to do so in the colder months.

What are the early signs and symptoms of antifreeze poisoning in cats (within 30 minutes to 12 hours)?

The early signs of ethylene glycol poisoning appear quickly, typically within 30 minutes to 3 hours of ingestion, and they are deceptive. At this stage, cats display what vets describe as a "drunk-like" state caused by the direct neurological effects of ethylene glycol before it has been fully metabolised into its most damaging by-products.

Early-stage symptoms to watch for include:

  • Appearing wobbly, uncoordinated, or stumbling (ataxia)
  • Seeming drowsy, dazed, or disorientated
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Excessive thirst and urination in the first few hours
  • Rapid eye movements (nystagmus)
  • Muscle twitching or mild tremors
  • Seeming unusually "out of it" or unresponsive to their name

A critical and dangerous feature of ethylene glycol poisoning is that these early signs often resolve on their own, typically within 6 to 12 hours, as the ethylene glycol is cleared from the bloodstream. According to guidance published by the British Small Animal Veterinary Association (BSAVA), many owners mistakenly believe their cat has recovered when in fact the most toxic metabolites are still forming and attacking the kidneys. This false recovery is one of the main reasons antifreeze poisoning so frequently proves fatal in cats. Treatment is most effective, and survival most likely, when given before the kidney damage phase begins.

What are the advanced symptoms and signs of acute kidney injury in cats?

Advanced-stage ethylene glycol poisoning represents a medical emergency with a very poor prognosis if treatment has not already begun. This stage typically develops between 12 and 24 hours after ingestion in cats. It progresses more rapidly in cats than in dogs, which is part of what makes feline cases so time-sensitive.

Signs of advancing kidney failure include:

  • Profound lethargy; the cat may be almost entirely unresponsive
  • Severe vomiting, sometimes continuous
  • Mouth ulcers and a distinctive ammonia-like smell on the breath (uraemic breath)
  • Swollen, painful kidneys, which a vet may be able to feel on examination
  • Reduced or absent urination as the kidneys shut down (oliguria or anuria)
  • Seizures
  • Coma

As Loyal & Loved explains, by the time a cat shows these advanced symptoms, the calcium oxalate crystals have already caused significant structural damage to the kidney tissue, and survival rates drop dramatically. Vets may still attempt supportive treatment at this stage, but owners must understand the prognosis is very guarded. Understanding the potential emergency vet treatment costs at this stage is something every cat owner should be prepared for.

What should you do immediately if you suspect antifreeze poisoning in your cat?

If you suspect your cat has ingested antifreeze, the single most important thing you can do is act immediately. Time is the decisive factor. Every minute spent waiting reduces the chance of a full recovery.

  1. Call your vet or an emergency vet right now. Do not wait to see if symptoms develop. Phone ahead so the practice can prepare treatment before you arrive.
  2. Do not induce vomiting at home. Unlike some other poisonings, inducing vomiting in cats should only be done by a vet using appropriate sedation and medication. Attempting this at home risks aspiration and causes significant distress.
  3. Try to identify the source. If you can safely identify what product was ingested, bring the packaging to the vet. This helps them confirm the active ingredient and choose the correct antidote.
  4. Note the time of suspected ingestion. This is critically important for treatment decisions, as vets calculate antidote dosing and expected metabolite levels based on how long ago exposure occurred.
  5. Keep your cat warm and calm. Wrap them gently in a towel or blanket and keep them still during transit to avoid worsening any neurological symptoms.
  6. Contact the Animal Poison Line (APL) if you cannot reach a vet immediately. The APL, operated in the UK by the VPIS, can be reached on 01202 509000. A per-case fee applies, typically around £35, but the advice can be lifesaving.

According to the VPIS, outcomes are significantly better when treatment begins within 3 hours of ingestion. After 5 to 6 hours in cats, the window for the most effective antidote therapy begins to close rapidly.

How do vets diagnose antifreeze poisoning in cats?

Veterinary diagnosis of ethylene glycol poisoning relies on a combination of clinical history, physical examination findings, and laboratory tests. A vet will begin by taking a history, including any known exposure, the timing of symptoms, and what products are kept in the household or nearby.

Diagnostic tests typically include:

  • Ethylene glycol test kit: Some veterinary practices carry a rapid in-house test kit that can detect ethylene glycol in blood within 30 minutes. This test is most reliable within the first few hours of ingestion. After approximately 6 to 8 hours, much of the ethylene glycol has been metabolised, and the test may return a false negative even in confirmed poisoning cases.
  • Blood biochemistry panel: This checks kidney values, specifically blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine, which rise as kidney function deteriorates. It also reveals electrolyte imbalances, particularly changes in calcium and phosphorus levels, and metabolic acidosis, a dangerous drop in blood pH caused by the toxic metabolites.
  • Complete blood count (CBC): Assesses overall health and signs of systemic stress or infection.
  • Urinalysis: A urine sample can reveal calcium oxalate crystals under a microscope. These crystals are a strongly suggestive sign of ethylene glycol toxicity. The urine may also fluoresce under ultraviolet light in the early hours after ingestion, because many antifreeze products contain sodium fluorescein as a leak-detecting dye, though this is not a definitive diagnostic test.
  • Blood gas analysis: Measures the degree of metabolic acidosis, which guides fluid therapy decisions.
  • Ultrasound: Kidney ultrasound may show increased echogenicity (brightness) in the kidney cortex, consistent with crystal deposition.

Loyal & Loved found that because of how quickly ethylene glycol is metabolised in cats, vets often have to make treatment decisions before all laboratory results are returned, based on clinical suspicion alone. If you have any reason to believe your cat has been exposed, tell your vet even if you are not certain.

What treatment options are available for antifreeze poisoning in cats?

Treatment for ethylene glycol poisoning is most effective when started as early as possible, ideally within 3 hours of ingestion. The goals of treatment are to prevent further metabolism of ethylene glycol into its toxic by-products, support the kidneys, correct metabolic disturbances, and manage complications.

Antidote therapy

The primary antidote for ethylene glycol poisoning is fomepizole (also known as 4-methylpyrazole or 4-MP). It works by blocking the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase, which is responsible for converting ethylene glycol into its toxic metabolites. According to a 2010 clinical study published in the Journal of Veterinary Emergency and Critical Care, fomepizole is highly effective in cats when given within 3 hours of ingestion, with survival rates of close to 100% when treatment begins in this window. However, its efficacy drops sharply after this point in cats, unlike in dogs where it remains effective for longer. Fomepizole is not widely stocked at all UK veterinary practices, so phoning ahead is essential.

Ethanol (medical-grade alcohol) was historically used as an alternative antidote in cats when fomepizole was unavailable, working on the same competitive inhibition principle. It carries more side effects and is now considered a second-line option.

Intravenous fluid therapy

Aggressive IV fluid therapy is a cornerstone of treatment. Fluids help to flush ethylene glycol and its metabolites from the body, correct metabolic acidosis, support blood pressure, and protect kidney function. Cats may require IV fluids for 24 to 72 hours or longer depending on severity.

Supportive care

Additional supportive measures include anti-nausea medication, sodium bicarbonate to correct acidosis, medications to manage seizures if they occur, and nutritional support. In cases where kidney failure has developed, dialysis may be considered at specialist referral centres, though this is not widely available across all UK veterinary practices and carries significant costs.

What are the likely costs?

Emergency antifreeze poisoning treatment in the UK is expensive, reflecting the intensive nursing and diagnostic work involved. Based on typical UK veterinary pricing in 2026, owners should anticipate costs in the range of £800 to £2,500 or more depending on the severity of the case, the length of hospitalisation, and whether referral to a specialist centre is needed. This is one of the strongest arguments for ensuring you have pet insurance to cover emergency vet costs before an emergency occurs. Policies from providers such as Itch Pet and Viovet offer options worth reviewing for this type of cover.

What is the recovery time, prognosis, and long-term care after antifreeze poisoning?

The prognosis for antifreeze poisoning in cats depends almost entirely on how quickly treatment was given relative to the time of ingestion. According to BSAVA guidance, cats treated with fomepizole within 3 hours of ingestion have an excellent prognosis. Those treated after kidney injury has begun face a guarded to poor prognosis, and some cats who survive the acute phase are left with permanent chronic kidney disease (CKD).

For cats who survive and recover full kidney function, the outlook is generally good. Most will require a hospital stay of 2 to 5 days and follow-up blood tests to confirm kidney values are returning to normal. Your vet will recommend follow-up checks at approximately 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months post-treatment.

For cats left with some degree of residual kidney damage, long-term management may include a kidney-supportive diet, increased water intake (wet food is strongly advised by the International Renal Interest Society, IRIS, in all CKD cats), and regular monitoring of kidney values. Foods formulated to support kidney health are available from specialist brands; your vet will advise on the most appropriate option for your cat's specific situation. Understanding the ongoing vet bills and pet health costs associated with CKD management is something to plan for if your cat is left with residual kidney disease.

How can cat owners prevent antifreeze poisoning?

Prevention is far simpler and kinder than treatment. Most antifreeze poisoning cases in UK cats are entirely preventable with a few consistent habits.

  • Store antifreeze securely: Keep all antifreeze, engine coolant, and screenwash products in a locked cabinet or out of any space your cat can access. Ensure lids are fully tightened after every use.
  • Clean up spills immediately and thoroughly: Even a small puddle on a garage floor or driveway is enough to be lethal. Use absorbent material to soak up spills and dispose of them safely. Rinse the area with plenty of water.
  • Check your vehicle for coolant leaks: A bright green, blue, or orange puddle beneath a vehicle is a warning sign. Have leaks repaired promptly.
  • Never use antifreeze in garden water features: This is a practice the RSPCA has specifically warned against. If you need to prevent garden water features from freezing, use safe alternatives (see below) or drain and switch them off over winter.
  • Supervise outdoor time in winter: Be aware of where your cat roams during the colder months. Neighbours' driveways and shared car parks are risk areas.
  • Choose safer products where possible: Opt for antifreeze products labelled as pet-safer formulations (see next section).
  • Wipe your cat's paws after outdoor time: This is particularly important if they have been walking on treated surfaces or near vehicles. A quick wipe prevents them ingesting residue during grooming.

During the peak winter months, seriously consider keeping cats indoors during winter, at least overnight and during early mornings when antifreeze spills from the night before may still be present on cold surfaces.

Are there safe antifreeze alternatives that are less toxic to cats?

Yes, safer alternatives do exist, though owners should understand that "safer" does not always mean completely harmless in large quantities. The main alternative to ethylene glycol-based antifreeze is propylene glycol.

Propylene glycol is a different alcohol compound that is metabolised by the body into non-toxic by-products (lactic acid rather than oxalic acid). It is approved as generally recognised as safe (GRAS) for many human food applications by the US Food and Drug Administration, and it has a significantly higher toxic threshold than ethylene glycol in mammals. According to peer-reviewed veterinary toxicology data, propylene glycol is approximately 6 to 7 times less toxic than ethylene glycol in cats. Large quantities can still cause problems, so it is not entirely without risk, but it represents a meaningfully safer choice for household use.

Propylene glycol-based antifreeze products are available in the UK, though they tend to be slightly more expensive than standard formulations, typically £8 to £15 per litre compared to £5 to £12 for ethylene glycol-based products. If you have cats and want to reduce risk meaningfully, it is worth making the switch. You can check price on Amazon for propylene glycol antifreeze products suitable for UK vehicles.

For garden water features, simply draining them over winter is the safest approach. If you must keep them running, use a small, specifically designed pond heater or de-icer rather than adding any glycol product to the water.

When should you call an emergency vet in the UK about antifreeze poisoning?

Call an emergency vet immediately in any of the following situations, without waiting for more symptoms to appear.

  • You have seen your cat lick or drink from a puddle that could be antifreeze or coolant.
  • You have found your cat near an open or spilled antifreeze container.
  • Your cat is showing any of the early neurological signs described above, including wobbling, vomiting, or unusual drowsiness, particularly during the winter months.
  • Your cat smells of something chemical or sweet that is unusual for them.
  • You simply suspect exposure, even without direct evidence. With this particular poison, acting on suspicion is always the right call.

In the UK in 2026, out-of-hours emergency vet services are widely available through dedicated emergency practices and OOH cover provided by many regular practices. Key services include Vets Now (operating across the UK with a network of emergency hospitals), Vets4Pets out-of-hours cover, and local independent emergency practices. If you do not already know which emergency service covers your area outside of regular hours, find out now, before an emergency happens. Keep the number saved in your phone.

The Animal Poison Line (APL) on 01202 509000 is also available 24 hours a day and can advise on risk assessment and next steps if you are unsure whether exposure has occurred. There is a per-case consultation fee of approximately £35, payable by card over the phone.

What are the UK regulations on antifreeze labelling and safe product disposal?

In the UK, antifreeze products containing ethylene glycol are classified as hazardous substances under the Classification, Labelling and Packaging (CLP) Regulation, which was retained in UK law following Brexit and is enforced by the Health and Safety Executive (HSE). Under these rules, products must carry appropriate hazard pictograms and risk phrases on their labels, including warnings relating to health hazards and environmental harm.

As of 2026, there is no UK law specifically requiring antifreeze to contain a bittering agent (a chemical that makes the product taste unpleasant) to deter animals from drinking it, unlike in some US states where such requirements exist. There have been calls from animal welfare organisations including the RSPCA and Cats Protection for mandatory bittering agents in UK antifreeze products, and some manufacturers do add them voluntarily, but it remains non-mandatory.

According to the Environment Agency, antifreeze and engine coolant are classified as hazardous waste in the UK and must not be poured down drains, into water courses, or onto the ground. The legal and recommended disposal method is to take used antifreeze to a local household waste recycling centre (HWRC), which will accept it for specialist disposal. Many local councils also accept small quantities of hazardous household waste at designated collection points. Pouring antifreeze down a drain is an offence under the Water Resources Act 1991 and can result in prosecution and fines.

Loyal & Loved found that awareness of these disposal rules is low among UK pet owners, despite the environmental and animal welfare risks. Antifreeze washed into gutters, drains, or garden areas can persist and pose a risk to cats, wildlife, and other animals.

Frequently Asked Questions

How quickly does antifreeze poisoning kill a cat?

Antifreeze poisoning can be fatal in cats within 24 to 72 hours of ingestion if left untreated. The speed of the progression makes it uniquely dangerous. Cats enter a phase of apparent recovery around 6 to 12 hours after ingestion, when the early neurological symptoms fade, but kidney damage is still advancing rapidly. Without treatment, acute kidney failure develops within 12 to 24 hours in most cats, and this is frequently fatal. This is why acting within the first 3 hours of suspected exposure is so critical.

Can a cat survive antifreeze poisoning?

Yes, cats can survive antifreeze poisoning if treatment is given quickly enough. According to veterinary toxicology research, survival rates approach 100% when the antidote fomepizole is administered within 3 hours of ethylene glycol ingestion. The prognosis worsens significantly as time passes, and cats who do not receive treatment, or who receive it after kidney failure has begun, face a very poor outlook. Some survivors are left with permanent chronic kidney disease requiring long-term management.

What does antifreeze poisoning look like in a cat?

In the first few hours, antifreeze poisoning in cats resembles drunkenness: the cat may stumble, appear disorientated, vomit, and seem unusually drowsy or unresponsive. These signs then appear to resolve, which is often mistaken for recovery. After 12 to 24 hours, signs of acute kidney failure develop, including severe lethargy, repeated vomiting, swollen and painful kidneys, reduced or absent urination, an ammonia-like smell on the breath, seizures, and coma. If your cat shows any of the early signs, especially in winter, contact a vet immediately without waiting for symptoms to worsen.

Is propylene glycol antifreeze safe to use around cats?

Propylene glycol-based antifreeze is significantly less toxic to cats than ethylene glycol-based antifreeze, roughly 6 to 7 times less toxic according to veterinary toxicology data. However, it is not completely harmless in large quantities and should still be stored securely and out of reach of cats. For household use where cats are present, propylene glycol formulations are a meaningfully safer choice. Always read the product label carefully before purchasing, as not all products advertised as "pet-friendly" are free from ethylene glycol.

What should I do if my cat licks antifreeze off the floor?

Call your vet or an emergency vet immediately. Do not wait to see if symptoms appear. Even a very small amount of ethylene glycol antifreeze can be lethal to a cat, and the most effective treatment window is within the first 3 hours. Note the time you believe the licking occurred and bring any product packaging with you to help the vet identify the active ingredient. If you cannot reach a vet, call the Animal Poison Line on 01202 509000.

Is antifreeze poisoning covered by cat insurance in the UK?

Most comprehensive cat insurance policies in the UK will cover emergency treatment for antifreeze poisoning, as it falls under unexpected illness and accident cover. The key is to have an active policy in place before an emergency occurs, as pre-existing conditions and incidents that occur before policy activation are typically excluded. Check your policy documents carefully for any exclusions relating to toxin ingestion. Reviewing a comparison of policies via a trusted resource is always a good starting point, and ensuring you have sufficient pet insurance to cover emergency vet costs is something every cat owner should prioritise before winter.
