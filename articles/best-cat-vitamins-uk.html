Most cats eating a complete, balanced commercial diet do not need vitamin supplements. However, cats with specific health conditions, older cats, or those on home-cooked or raw diets may genuinely benefit from targeted supplementation. This guide cuts through the noise to tell you exactly which supplements have evidence behind them, which UK products are worth buying in 2026, and when to leave well alone.

Olive the tabby cat would like you to know she is deeply suspicious of anything in tablet form. She is, however, grudgingly accepting of a salmon-flavoured paste squeezed onto her paw. We will come back to that.

Do Cats Really Need Supplements?

The honest answer is: most cats eating a complete diet do not need additional supplements, and giving unnecessary ones can cause harm. According to the British Small Animal Veterinary Association (BSAVA), a diet labelled "complete" must by law meet all of a cat's nutritional requirements under the EU-derived retained legislation that continues to govern UK pet food standards in 2026. If your cat is eating a quality complete food, such as those covered in our guide to the best cat food UK, supplementation is unlikely to add any benefit.

That said, there are genuine exceptions. According to Loyal & Loved, the cats most likely to benefit from supplements fall into four groups: senior cats (typically aged 11 and over), cats recovering from illness or surgery, cats on veterinary elimination or home-cooked diets, and cats with specific diagnosed conditions such as joint disease, chronic kidney disease, or poor coat quality.

Over-supplementation is a real risk. Fat-soluble vitamins A and D, in particular, accumulate in the body and can reach toxic levels. A 2019 review published in the Journal of Nutritional Science confirmed that vitamin A toxicity (hypervitaminosis A) is well documented in cats fed liver-heavy diets or inappropriate supplements. More is emphatically not better.

Key Nutrients Cats Cannot Make Themselves: What Are the Real Essentials?

Cats are obligate carnivores, meaning they depend entirely on animal-derived nutrients to survive. Several nutrients that dogs and humans can synthesise internally must come entirely from a cat's diet or supplementation.

Taurine

Taurine is an amino acid (a building block of protein) that cats cannot synthesise in sufficient quantities from other amino acids. According to the American Association of Feed Control Officials (AAFCO), whose standards inform UK pet food formulation, taurine deficiency causes dilated cardiomyopathy (an enlarged, weakened heart) and retinal degeneration leading to blindness. All reputable complete cat foods include taurine, but cats on raw or home-cooked diets may be at risk if the recipes are not properly balanced.

Arachidonic Acid

This is an omega-6 fatty acid that cats, unlike dogs, cannot convert from linoleic acid. It must come from animal fat. Deficiency affects skin integrity, reproduction, and immune function. Again, complete commercial foods cover this, but home-prepared diets often fall short.

Vitamin A (Preformed)

Cats cannot convert beta-carotene from plant sources into vitamin A. They need preformed vitamin A (retinol) from animal sources such as liver. As Loyal & Loved explains, this is one reason why giving a cat a vitamin A supplement on top of a complete diet is genuinely dangerous: it is very easy to tip into toxic territory.

Niacin (Vitamin B3)

Cats have a higher metabolic requirement for niacin than most mammals and cannot synthesise enough from tryptophan. Deficiency causes weight loss, poor coat condition, and neurological signs. Niacin is present in meat and fish, so cats eating animal-based complete diets are generally protected.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA and DHA)

Unlike humans, cats convert plant-based ALA omega-3 very poorly into the biologically useful forms EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid). These are anti-inflammatory fatty acids found in oily fish and fish oil, and they support joint health, brain function, skin quality, and heart health. This is one area where supplementation can genuinely add value, particularly for cats eating dry kibble diets where fish oil content may have degraded.

Supplements for Senior Cats: What Changes as They Age?

Senior cats, generally considered to be those aged 11 and over, have different nutritional needs to younger adults. According to the International Cat Care (iCatCare) organisation, common age-related changes include reduced kidney function, decreased muscle mass, joint stiffness, and a decline in digestive efficiency. Some of these changes can be meaningfully supported with targeted supplementation.

B Vitamins

Older cats, particularly those with early-stage chronic kidney disease (CKD), lose water-soluble B vitamins through increased urination. A B-complex supplement or a diet formulated for senior cats with higher B vitamin levels can help maintain energy metabolism and nerve function. Vetri-Science B-Complex is available through Viovet at approximately £14 to £18.

Phosphorus and Kidney Support

Phosphorus restriction is a cornerstone of managing CKD in cats, and phosphorus binders such as Pronefra (available on veterinary prescription, approximately £25 to £35 per bottle) are used under vet supervision. Do not attempt to manage kidney disease with over-the-counter supplements alone; this requires veterinary oversight.

Antioxidants

Vitamins C and E act as antioxidants, helping to neutralise free radicals that accumulate with age. Some senior cat foods already include elevated levels, but a supplement such as Coyne Healthcare VetActives Antioxidant (around £16 to £20) can be useful for cats on standard adult diets who have not yet moved to a senior formula.

As Loyal & Loved explains, the single most important step for a senior cat is a six-monthly vet health check rather than a supplement cabinet. Blood work identifying early kidney or thyroid changes will always be more valuable than any supplement bought without a diagnosis.

Supplements for Cats with Joint Problems: Do Glucosamine and Chondroitin Actually Work?

Joint supplements for cats are one of the most purchased categories, but the evidence is more nuanced than many manufacturers suggest. Osteoarthritis (OA) is common in cats, with a 2011 study in the Veterinary Journal finding radiographic evidence of joint disease in over 90% of cats aged 12 and over.

Glucosamine and Chondroitin

Glucosamine (an amino sugar) and chondroitin (a structural component of cartilage) are the most widely used joint supplements. Evidence in cats is limited compared to dogs, but a 2007 study in the Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine found moderate evidence for improved mobility and reduced pain scores in cats with OA given these compounds. They are generally considered safe at recommended doses.

Cosequin for Cats is one of the best-known products in this space. A 60-capsule pack costs approximately £22 to £28 and is available through Viovet. The capsules can be opened and the powder mixed into wet food.

Green-Lipped Mussel Extract

Green-lipped mussel (Perna canaliculus) is a New Zealand shellfish that contains both omega-3 fatty acids and naturally occurring glycosaminoglycans (joint-supporting compounds). YuMOVE Cat contains green-lipped mussel extract and costs approximately £18 to £23 for a 60-tablet pack. It is one of the few cat joint supplements with a Veterinary Health Approval.

Omega-3 for Joint Inflammation

EPA and DHA from fish oil have anti-inflammatory properties that can reduce the pain and swelling associated with arthritis. A 2010 study in the Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association demonstrated that fish oil supplementation reduced lameness scores in dogs with OA; while the cat-specific data is thinner, the mechanism is the same and many vets recommend fish oil as a safe, low-cost adjunct to joint management.

It is worth noting that supplements work alongside, not instead of, environmental modifications for arthritic cats. Lower-sided litter trays, ramps to favourite sleeping spots, and heated beds all make a measurable difference. Your cat's indoor vs outdoor cat health differences will also affect how much environmental support they need.

Supplements for Skin and Coat Health: What Actually Improves a Cat's Coat?

A dull, flaky, or greasy coat in a cat is usually a symptom of an underlying issue rather than a simple nutrient deficiency. Before reaching for a supplement, it is worth ruling out parasites, allergies, dental pain (which prevents grooming), obesity (which prevents a cat reaching all areas), or hyperthyroidism. That said, omega-3 fatty acids and biotin have genuine evidence for supporting skin and coat quality.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids

EPA and DHA reduce inflammatory skin conditions and improve coat gloss and texture. A 2016 review in Veterinary Dermatology found that omega-3 supplementation significantly improved coat condition and reduced pruritus (itching) in cats with allergic skin disease. The dose typically used in clinical practice is 20 to 55mg EPA and DHA per kilogram of body weight per day, so for a 4kg cat, approximately 80 to 220mg combined EPA and DHA daily.

Biotin (Vitamin B7)

Biotin supports keratin production (keratin is the protein that makes up fur and skin). Deficiency is rare in cats on complete diets but can occur in cats eating large amounts of raw egg white, which contains avidin, a compound that blocks biotin absorption. Biotin supplements such as those in the YuMEGA Cat range are low-risk and inexpensive.

Evening Primrose Oil

Evening primrose oil contains gamma-linolenic acid (GLA), an omega-6 fatty acid with anti-inflammatory properties. Some studies in dogs and humans suggest benefit for atopic dermatitis, and it is included in several UK cat skin supplements. The evidence in cats specifically is limited, but the risk profile is low at recommended doses.

Loyal & Loved found that for most cats with coat concerns, switching to a higher-quality, higher-meat-content complete food such as those from AATU Dog & Cat Food or KatKin produced more visible improvement than any supplement added to a lower-quality diet. Get the diet right first.

How to Give Your Cat Supplements Without a Struggle: Practical Methods That Actually Work

Getting a cat to accept a supplement requires patience, cunning, and a willingness to try several approaches before finding the one that works. Olive the tabby has field-tested all of the following methods and approves of approximately two of them.

Liquid Supplements Added to Wet Food

This is the easiest method for most cats. Liquid fish oil or vitamin drops added to a small amount of strong-smelling wet food (tuna-based foods work particularly well) are accepted by the majority of cats. Start with a tiny amount and increase gradually over a week to avoid immediate rejection.

Paste Supplements on the Paw

Many supplement pastes can be applied to the back of the cat's front paw. Cats will almost always groom it off, ensuring they consume the dose. Products like Beaphar Kitty Milk Paste (which contains vitamins and minerals) are formulated specifically for this method and cost around £5 to £8 per tube from Viovet.

Capsules Opened into Food

Many supplement capsules can be unscrewed or pierced and the contents mixed into wet food. Always check the manufacturer's guidance first, as some capsule contents are designed for delayed release and should not be opened.

Pill Pockets or Treats

If a supplement comes in tablet form, pill pocket treats (soft pouches designed to conceal tablets) are useful. GREENIES Pill Pockets for Cats cost approximately £6 to £8 for a pack of 45 from Amazon UK. The success rate with cats is lower than with dogs, but for some individuals it works perfectly.

Pill Giver / Piller

As a last resort, a plastic pill giver (a small syringe-like device that places the tablet at the back of the throat) can be effective. Ask your vet to demonstrate the technique first. Always follow immediately with a syringe of water (around 5ml) to ensure the tablet reaches the stomach, as dry tablets lodged in the oesophagus can cause ulceration.

When to Speak to Your Vet Before Supplementing: Are There Situations Where Supplements Are Dangerous?

Yes, there are several situations where supplementing without veterinary guidance carries genuine risk. According to the Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD), supplements classified as complementary feedingstuffs fall outside the licensing requirements of veterinary medicines, which means quality control and dosing standards can vary considerably between products.

Cats with Kidney Disease

Many supplements, including certain B vitamins and phosphorus-containing products, require careful management in cats with CKD. High doses of vitamin D are particularly dangerous as they can raise blood calcium levels and worsen kidney function. Always consult your vet before supplementing a cat with known kidney disease.

Cats on Prescription Medication

Some supplements interact with medications. Fish oil, for example, has mild anticoagulant properties and can interact with drugs like prednisolone or meloxicam. Herbal supplements, including valerian and chamomile marketed for cat anxiety, can affect liver enzyme metabolism. Always disclose any supplements to your vet when medication is prescribed.

Cats with Hyperthyroidism

Iodine content in some supplements, particularly those containing seaweed or kelp, can interfere with hyperthyroidism management, including the prescription iodine-restricted diet Hill's y/d. This is a situation where even a seemingly harmless supplement can derail treatment.

Pregnant or Lactating Cats

Vitamin A supplementation in pregnant queens carries a risk of foetal abnormalities. Do not supplement a pregnant cat without explicit veterinary advice.

As Loyal & Loved explains, the safest starting point is always a vet conversation. A ten-minute consultation, which typically costs £30 to £60 at a UK practice in 2026, is money far better spent than a shelf full of supplements chosen at random. Many practices also offer nurse consultations at lower cost, which are appropriate for straightforward nutritional queries.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do indoor cats need more vitamins than outdoor cats?

Not necessarily more vitamins, but indoor cats may have different needs. They are less likely to supplement their diet by hunting, which can mean lower taurine intake if they are eating a plant-heavy or low-quality diet. They may also have less exposure to sunlight, though cats synthesise vitamin D primarily through diet rather than sunlight, unlike humans. Indoor cats are more prone to obesity, which can affect nutrient metabolism. For a full breakdown, see our guide to indoor vs outdoor cat health differences.

What is the best vitamin supplement for cats in the UK?

For cats on a complete commercial diet, the supplement with the broadest genuine evidence base is an omega-3 fish oil supplement such as Iceland Pure Salmon Oil (around £12 to £15) or YuMOVE PLUS Cat (around £18 to £22). For cats on home-cooked or raw diets, a comprehensive supplement mix such as Feline Instincts My Natural Cat is essential. All are available through Viovet. There is no single best supplement for all cats; the right choice depends on your cat's age, diet, and health status.

Is taurine deficiency common in UK cats?

Taurine deficiency is uncommon in cats eating complete commercial diets, as AAFCO and European FEDIAF guidelines require adequate taurine inclusion. It is a genuine risk for cats eating home-cooked diets, particularly those based on chicken or beef without organ meat, or diets that rely heavily on plant protein. If your cat eats a home-prepared diet, consult a veterinary nutritionist and ensure taurine supplementation is included. A taurine supplement such as NOW Foods Taurine Powder costs approximately £12 to £18 per 227g tub on Amazon UK.

Can I give my cat human vitamins or fish oil capsules?

Human fish oil capsules (omega-3 only, no added vitamins) are generally safe for cats at appropriate doses and are often cheaper than pet-specific products. However, human multivitamins must never be given to cats. Many contain vitamin D at levels toxic to cats, and some include xylitol (an artificial sweetener) which, while less well-studied in cats than in dogs, is best avoided entirely. Always choose a product formulated for cats or a single-ingredient supplement such as plain fish oil, and confirm the dose with your vet.

How long does it take for cat supplements to work?

This varies considerably by supplement type. Omega-3 fatty acids typically show visible coat improvement within six to eight weeks of consistent daily use. Joint supplements such as glucosamine and chondroitin may take eight to twelve weeks before any mobility improvement is noticeable, and not all cats respond. Probiotics for acute digestive upset can show benefit within three to five days. If you have seen no improvement after three months of consistent supplementation at the correct dose, discuss alternatives with your vet.
