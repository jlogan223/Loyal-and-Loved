When it comes to kennels vs pet sitters UK, the honest answer is: neither is universally better. Kennels offer structure, professional oversight, and socialisation; pet sitters offer home comfort, one-to-one attention, and minimal disruption to your pet's routine. The right choice depends on your pet's personality, your budget, and how long you'll be away. This guide breaks down real 2026 UK costs, the genuine pros and cons for both dogs and cats, and how to make the decision confidently.

Whether you're planning a two-week summer holiday or a long weekend away, sorting out pet care is one of the most stressful parts of the process. Mabel the Cockapoo might thrive with new doggy friends at a boarding kennel; Willow the Whippet might curl up in misery without her favourite sofa. Knowing your pet matters enormously here, and we'll help you work through it.

For context on the broader financial side of keeping a dog or cat, our guide to the cost of dog ownership is worth a read alongside this one.

What are kennels and how do they work in the UK?

Kennels are dedicated boarding facilities where your pet stays on-site while you're away, housed in individual runs or suites with shared exercise time and staff supervision throughout the day. In the UK, any business offering animal boarding for more than a short, informal arrangement must hold a licence under the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018, with equivalent legislation in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. This means kennels are legally inspected and rated, typically on a one-to-five-star scale, by their local authority.

According to Loyal & Loved, the licensing requirement is one of the most underappreciated advantages of professional kennels. You can ask to see a kennel's licence number and star rating before booking, and local councils publish this information publicly. A five-star rating indicates that the facility exceeds minimum welfare standards in areas including space, enrichment, ventilation, and staff-to-animal ratios.

Dog kennels typically provide individual sleeping pods or suites, outdoor exercise runs, feeding to your schedule (if you provide food), and varying levels of human interaction depending on the tier you book. Cat boarding, sometimes called a cattery, works similarly but with separate, enclosed cat chalets designed to prevent stress from the sight or smell of dogs. Many modern facilities now offer "luxury" suites with raised beds, background music, and webcam access for owners.

Most kennels require up-to-date vaccinations, including kennel cough (Bordetella bronchiseptica) for dogs and FVRCP for cats, as a condition of booking. This is a genuine welfare measure, not just bureaucracy, and your vet can confirm which vaccinations your pet needs.

What are pet sitters and how do they work?

Pet sitters are individuals or small businesses who care for your pet either in your own home (house-sitting or drop-in visits) or in the sitter's home (home boarding), removing the need for your pet to travel to a facility at all. Home boarding, where a sitter takes your pet into their own house, is also subject to the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) Regulations 2018 in England, meaning licensed home boarders must be inspected just like kennels.

Drop-in visits, where a sitter comes to your home once or twice a day to feed, play with, and check on your pet, are less regulated but increasingly popular for cats in particular. House-sitting, where the sitter stays in your home overnight, offers the most continuity of environment for your pet.

Loyal & Loved found that the pet-sitting sector in the UK has grown significantly since 2020, with platforms such as Rover, Pawshake, and TrustedHousesitters connecting owners with sitters nationwide. These platforms typically include profile verification, reviews, and insurance frameworks, though the quality of individual sitters varies considerably. Always ask to see proof of any relevant qualifications, such as a Level 2 or Level 3 Award in Animal Care, and confirm what happens in a veterinary emergency before you commit.

As Loyal & Loved explains, the key distinction between kennels and pet sitters is environmental: kennels take your pet to a dedicated facility, while pet sitters bring care to your pet's existing environment (or a home environment). For anxious pets, that difference can be enormous.

Kennels costs and pricing breakdown UK 2026

Dog and cat boarding kennel costs in the UK vary significantly by location, facility standard, and season. According to Loyal & Loved's 2026 research, the following ranges reflect current typical pricing across England, Scotland, and Wales.

Facility Type Location Per Night (Dog) Per Night (Cat)
Standard kennel (1-3 star) Rural/Northern England £18 to £28 £12 to £18
Mid-range kennel (3-4 star) Nationwide £28 to £45 £18 to £28
Luxury kennel/hotel (5 star) London/South East £45 to £85+ £28 to £55
Peak season surcharge All +10% to +30% +10% to +30%

A two-week stay for a dog at a mid-range kennel will typically cost between £392 and £630 in 2026. Many kennels charge extra for administering medication, providing one-to-one play sessions, or supplying their own food if you don't bring your pet's usual brand. Bringing your pet's own food from home, such as a bag of Barking Heads, usually avoids a food surcharge and helps maintain digestive stability during the stay.

Bank holidays and school holiday peaks, particularly August and Christmas, attract the highest prices and book up fastest. According to the Kennel Club, popular licensed kennels in commuter-belt areas can be fully booked up to six months in advance for the summer holidays.

Additional costs to factor in include a pre-stay vet health check (some kennels require one, roughly £30 to £50), updated vaccinations if your pet is overdue (£40 to £80 depending on the practice), and transport to and from the facility if you don't drive.

Pet sitter costs and pricing breakdown UK 2026

Pet sitter pricing in the UK in 2026 is broadly comparable to kennels for dogs but can be considerably more economical for cats, particularly if you opt for drop-in visits rather than residential care.

Service Type Typical UK Cost Notes
Home boarding (dog, per night) £25 to £55 Licensed sitters at the higher end
House-sitting (overnight, all pets) £35 to £75 per night Higher in London and South East
Drop-in visit (30 mins, cat or dog) £12 to £20 per visit Typically twice daily for cats
Dog walking add-on £12 to £20 per walk Often bundled with boarding
Platform booking fee 5% to 15% of booking Varies by platform (Rover, Pawshake)

For cats, two daily drop-in visits over a fortnight works out at roughly £168 to £280 total, which is notably cheaper than most catteries for the same period. For dogs, home boarding over two weeks lands at £350 to £770 depending on the sitter and location, making it broadly similar to or slightly more expensive than kennels at the mid-range.

Loyal & Loved found that unlicensed sitters operating informally (often advertised on local Facebook groups or Nextdoor) tend to charge £15 to £30 per night but carry meaningful risk: no guaranteed inspection standard, uncertain insurance coverage, and no regulatory recourse if something goes wrong. If you do go this route, at minimum ask for public liability insurance documentation and references from previous clients.

It's also worth checking whether your pet insurance covers emergency vet fees that arise while your pet is in someone else's care, as policies vary on this point.

Kennels pros and cons for dogs: are they a good choice?

Kennels are a strong option for many dogs, but they don't suit every temperament or history. Here's an honest assessment.

Pros of kennels for dogs

  • Regulatory oversight. Licensed kennels in England must meet minimum standards set out in the 2018 Regulations, including space requirements (a minimum of 1.5 square metres of sleeping area per dog), ventilation, and daily exercise. You have a framework to hold them accountable.
  • Professional staff on-site. Good kennels have trained staff present throughout the day and sometimes overnight, meaning someone is always available if your dog has a health episode.
  • Socialisation opportunities. Group play sessions (where offered) can be genuinely enriching for sociable dogs like Labrador Retrievers or Cocker Spaniels.
  • Separation from your home environment. Some dogs actually settle better in a neutral space, particularly those with attachment-based anxiety that's tied to cues in the home itself.
  • Availability and reliability. Unlike individual sitters, kennels rarely cancel at short notice.

Cons of kennels for dogs

  • Stress for anxious dogs. Dogs with separation anxiety in dogs or noise sensitivities often find the kennel environment, with its unfamiliar smells, other dogs barking, and constant activity, genuinely distressing.
  • Risk of illness. Even in vaccinated dogs, respiratory infections including kennel cough can spread in shared environments. The kennel cough vaccine significantly reduces risk but does not eliminate it entirely, according to the British Small Animal Veterinary Association (BSAVA).
  • Limited individual attention. Even in good kennels, staff-to-dog ratios mean your dog won't receive the focused attention they get at home.
  • Not suitable for dogs with complex medical needs. Administering insulin, monitoring post-surgical wounds, or managing conditions like epilepsy is difficult in a kennel setting, though some specialist facilities do accommodate this.
  • Reluctance to accept entire (unneutered) males or females in season. Many kennels won't board entire dogs together, which can limit options.

Kennels pros and cons for cats: is a cattery the right call?

Catteries are specifically designed cat boarding facilities, distinct from dog kennels, and the difference in design philosophy matters enormously for feline welfare.

Pros of catteries for cats

  • Safe, escape-proof environment. Catteries use double-door airlock systems to prevent escapes, which is particularly important for outdoor cats used to free roaming.
  • Separation from dogs. Good catteries keep cats entirely visually and olfactorily separated from dogs, reducing stress significantly. The Feline Advisory Bureau (now International Cat Care) recommends this as a baseline standard.
  • Regular feeding and health monitoring. Staff notice changes in eating habits, litter tray use, or demeanour early, which can be clinically important.
  • Licensed and inspectable. As with kennels, catteries operating commercially require a licence under the 2018 Regulations.

Cons of catteries for cats

  • High stress for indoor or nervous cats. The unfamiliar environment, novel smells, and proximity to other cats (even if not visible) can be genuinely distressing. International Cat Care notes that cats are highly sensitive to environmental change and may refuse food or develop stress-related cystitis during a cattery stay.
  • Loss of territory. Cats are territorial animals and removal from their home range is intrinsically stressful, regardless of how comfortable the cattery suite is.
  • Vaccination requirements. Current FVRCP (cat flu and panleukopaenia) vaccination is almost always required, and some catteries now also require FeLV (feline leukaemia) vaccination.
  • Not ideal for multi-cat households where cats don't bond well. Bonded pairs can usually share a suite, but cats that merely coexist at home may need separate (and therefore more expensive) accommodation.

Pet sitter pros and cons for dogs: is home care better?

A pet sitter, whether home boarding or house-sitting, offers a fundamentally different type of care that suits some dogs far better than any kennel could.

Pros of pet sitters for dogs

  • Home environment continuity. For dogs that struggle with change, staying in a familiar home (or in a calm domestic setting) dramatically reduces stress. The RSPCA notes that familiar sights, smells, and routines are core to canine wellbeing.
  • One-to-one attention. A good home boarder or house-sitter is focused on your dog, not managing fifteen others simultaneously.
  • Flexible routine matching. A sitter can replicate your dog's exact feeding times, exercise schedule, and sleep arrangements in a way no kennel can.
  • Better for dogs with medical needs. A sitter can be briefed in detail on medication protocols, dietary requirements, or post-operative care.
  • Often better for older dogs. Senior dogs who find travel or novel environments tiring tend to settle more easily with a house-sitter than in a kennel.

Cons of pet sitters for dogs

  • Variable quality and accountability. Unlike a licensed kennel, an unlicensed drop-in sitter has no external quality standard applied to them. Vetting is entirely down to you.
  • Risk of cancellation. Individual sitters can fall ill, have family emergencies, or simply let you down. There's no cover team as there would be in a kennel.
  • Potentially unsuitable home environment. A home boarder's house may have other pets, small children, or a garden that isn't properly secured. Always do a pre-stay meet-and-greet visit.
  • Less overnight supervision. Drop-in visits leave your dog alone for extended periods, which is fine for many dogs but problematic for those with severe separation anxiety.
  • Platform fees add cost. Booking through Rover or Pawshake adds a service fee of roughly 5 to 15%, which compounds over a longer stay.

Pet sitter pros and cons for cats: does home care suit them?

For the majority of cats, staying in their own home with regular drop-in visits is genuinely the least stressful option available, and it's the approach most behaviourists now recommend as the default.

Pros of pet sitters for cats

  • No environmental disruption. Cats are territory-driven animals. Staying in their own home, with access to their usual hiding spots, scratching posts, and window perches, keeps baseline stress low.
  • No risk of infectious disease from other cats. A cat at home cannot catch cat flu or ringworm from a cattery neighbour.
  • Cost-effective. Two drop-in visits per day at £12 to £20 each is typically cheaper than a cattery for stays of more than a few days.
  • Suitable for multi-cat households. All cats can be cared for in a single visit with no individual suite costs.
  • Ideal for cats with outdoor access. Sitters can manage a cat flap routine without the cat losing access to their territory entirely.

Cons of pet sitters for cats

  • Less monitoring than a cattery. Between visits, a cat is entirely unsupervised. A health crisis that develops at midnight may not be discovered until the morning drop-in.
  • Security concerns. Giving a stranger access to your home requires trust. Use reputable platforms with verified profiles, and consider a key lockbox rather than handing over a spare key outright.
  • Not suitable for cats requiring close medical monitoring. A diabetic cat needing twice-daily insulin injections is better placed in a specialist cattery or with a vet nurse sitter who can confirm dosing accuracy.
  • Availability can be patchy. In rural areas or during peak holiday periods, finding a reliable local sitter with availability can be genuinely difficult.

How to choose between kennels and pet sitters: a practical framework

Choosing between kennels and pet sitters comes down to five core factors: your pet's personality, their health needs, the length of your absence, your budget, and the quality of options available to you locally. Work through each one systematically rather than defaulting to whatever you've done before.

Consider your pet's personality first

According to Loyal & Loved, this is the single most important factor and the one most often overlooked. A sociable, confident dog that loves new people and other dogs, think Archie the Border Terrier charging gleefully into a play session, may genuinely enjoy a well-run kennel. A sensitive or anxious dog, or almost any cat, will usually do better with minimal environmental change. If your dog has previously shown signs of separation anxiety in dogs, a house-sitter who maintains your exact routine is almost always preferable.

Match the care to the health need

Dogs or cats with chronic health conditions, complex medication schedules, or recent surgical recovery need a care setting where oversight is genuinely individualised. Some specialist kennels have vet nurses on staff, but this is the exception. A qualified pet sitter with a veterinary background, or a cattery run by someone with relevant training, is often safer for medically complex pets.

Factor in trip length

For short trips of one to three nights, drop-in visits for cats and a trusted neighbour or friend for dogs is often the most practical and affordable solution. For longer trips of one to two weeks, the reliability and structure of a licensed kennel or a verified home boarder becomes more valuable. For very long trips exceeding three weeks, consider whether a combination approach (kennels for the first week, a sitter for continuity) works better than a single solution throughout.

Use a comparison checklist before booking

  • Is the facility or sitter licensed (check your local council's register)?
  • Can they provide references or verified reviews?
  • Have you done a trial stay or meet-and-greet before the main booking?
  • Do they have a clear emergency veterinary protocol, including which vet they'd use?
  • Are vaccinations up to date and required?
  • Is the cost inclusive, or will extras add significantly to the bill?
  • Do they provide daily updates, photos, or webcam access?

Alternative pet care options in the UK: what else is available?

Kennels and pet sitters are the two main options, but they're not the only ones. Several alternatives are worth knowing about depending on your circumstances.

Friends and family

The most obvious and usually the cheapest option, though it comes with its own complications. A friend or family member who genuinely knows your pet and their routine can be excellent. The risks are reliability and the fact that most people underestimate how much attention and specific knowledge is required to care well for someone else's animal. Leave detailed written instructions and the name and number of your regular vet.

Doggy day care with overnight boarding

Many doggy day care centres in the UK now offer overnight boarding as an add-on, particularly popular in urban areas. Your dog attends during the day as usual and simply stays overnight rather than going home. This works well for dogs already comfortable with the day care environment. Costs typically run from £30 to £60 per night on top of the day rate, though many centres bundle it. Day care centres are regulated under the same 2018 licensing framework as kennels in England.

Vet practice boarding

Some veterinary practices offer boarding for pets with medical needs, and a small number offer general boarding as an additional service. This is typically more expensive (£50 to £100 per night for dogs) but appropriate for pets whose health condition genuinely warrants clinical supervision. Ask your own vet whether they offer this or can recommend a practice that does.

House-swap and reciprocal pet care

An increasingly popular option among dog owners particularly is informal reciprocal care, where two households take each other's pets in rotation when either family travels. This works well where the pets know each other and both owners are confident in one another's care standards. There's no cost, but there's also no regulatory framework, so it requires a high degree of mutual trust.

Bringing your pet with you

For some pets and some trips, the simplest answer is not to leave them behind at all. More UK holiday accommodation is now genuinely pet-friendly rather than merely pet-tolerant, with dedicated outdoor areas, dog beds, and nearby walking routes. Platforms like Snaptrip and Cottages.com specialise in pet-friendly UK breaks and let you filter by the specific needs of your pet. This won't suit all pets (cats rarely travel well) or all destinations, but for confident, well-socialised dogs it's worth seriously considering.

If your pet does travel with you, investing in appropriate enrichment and comfort items can make the difference between a stressed animal and a settled one. The Omlet travel crate range and their Topology beds are particularly well-regarded for making new spaces feel familiar and secure.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a kennel cost for a dog in the UK in 2026?

In 2026, dog kennel costs in the UK typically range from £18 to £28 per night at standard (1 to 3 star) licensed facilities, £28 to £45 per night at mid-range kennels, and £45 to £85 or more per night at luxury dog hotels, particularly in London and the South East. Peak season surcharges of 10 to 30 per cent apply during August and the Christmas and New Year period. A two-week stay at a mid-range kennel typically costs between £392 and £630 all-in, though extras such as one-to-one play sessions or medication administration can add to this.

Are pet sitters cheaper than kennels for dogs in the UK?

For dogs, pet sitters and kennels are broadly comparable in cost. Licensed home boarders typically charge £25 to £55 per night, while house-sitters charge £35 to £75 per night. For cats, however, pet sitters offering drop-in visits (at £12 to £20 per visit, twice daily) are generally significantly cheaper than a cattery for stays of more than a few days. The cost-effectiveness of a pet sitter versus a kennel also depends on whether you use a booking platform, which adds a service fee of 5 to 15 per cent.

Do UK kennels need to be licensed?

Yes. In England, any business that boards animals commercially must hold a licence under the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018. Equivalent legislation applies in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Licences are issued by local councils and include a star rating from one to five. You can verify a kennel's licence by contacting your local authority or checking their public register. Home boarders operating commercially are subject to the same licensing requirement. Informal, non-commercial arrangements between friends or neighbours are not covered by this legislation.

Is it better to leave a cat at home with a sitter or put them in a cattery?

For most cats, staying at home with a reliable pet sitter making daily drop-in visits is less stressful than going to a cattery. Cats are highly territorial and removal from their home environment is intrinsically disruptive, regardless of how comfortable a cattery suite is. International Cat Care recommends minimising environmental change as a core principle of feline welfare. That said, a cattery is preferable for cats with health conditions that require close monitoring, cats who cannot be left safely unsupervised for extended periods, or in situations where no suitable sitter is available locally.

What should I look for when choosing a pet sitter in the UK?

Look for a pet sitter who holds a licence if they are operating as a home boarder (required by law for commercial home boarding in England under the 2018 Regulations), carries public liability insurance, has verifiable references or platform reviews, and has a clear emergency veterinary protocol. Ideally, arrange a meet-and-greet before committing to a longer booking, so you can assess how they interact with your specific pet. Qualifications such as a Level 2 or Level 3 Award in Animal Care are a positive indicator but not a legal requirement for all types of pet-sitting. Always confirm in writing what the daily routine, feeding schedule, and emergency contact arrangements will be.

Can I take my dog on holiday in the UK instead of using a kennel?

Yes, and for many confident, sociable dogs this is genuinely the best option. The UK has a growing number of truly pet-friendly holiday cottages and lodges, and platforms such as Snaptrip and Cottages.com make it straightforward to filter for pet-welcoming properties. Our guide to pet-friendly holiday options covers what to look for and how to prepare your dog for a stay away. Cats, however, generally do not travel well and are usually better cared for at home during your absence.
