The best food for cocker spaniels in the UK in 2026 is one that is high in named animal protein, moderate in fat, free from artificial additives, and gentle enough for a breed that is genuinely prone to digestive sensitivity. Cocker spaniels, whether English or Working type, thrive on diets where the first ingredient is a real, identifiable meat source rather than a cereal or a vague "meat and animal derivatives" catch-all. Get the diet right and you will notice the difference in their coat, their energy levels, and yes, those notorious ears.

This guide covers the best dry food, wet food, and fresh food options available in the UK right now, with honest assessments of cost, quality, and suitability at every life stage. Whether you are feeding a bouncy cocker spaniel puppy, a working dog covering miles of field, or a slightly greying older dog who has earned the sofa permanently, there is a recommendation here for you.

What Are a Cocker Spaniel's Nutritional Needs?

Cocker spaniels need a diet that balances energy, coat health, and digestive comfort. According to the European Pet Food Industry Federation (FEDIAF) 2025 nutritional guidelines, adult dogs generally require a minimum of 18% crude protein on a dry matter basis, but active breeds like working cockers benefit from 25 to 30% or above. Cocker spaniels have a moderately high energy requirement relative to their size, particularly the working type, which can cover enormous distances during a day in the field.

Key nutrients to look for in a cocker spaniel's diet include:

  • High-quality animal protein: Supports lean muscle and a healthy coat. Look for named sources such as chicken, salmon, or duck listed first on the ingredient panel.
  • Omega-3 and Omega-6 fatty acids: Essential for the cocker spaniel's famously glossy coat. Salmon oil, herring, and flaxseed are good sources.
  • Easily digestible carbohydrates: Sweet potato, brown rice, and oats are gentler on sensitive digestive systems than wheat or corn.
  • Prebiotics and probiotics: Help maintain gut flora balance, which is particularly important for a breed with a tendency towards loose stools.
  • Zinc and Biotin: Support skin and coat health, reducing the likelihood of the dry, flaky skin some cockers experience.
  • Adequate fibre: Supports regular, firm stools and healthy anal gland function, a common concern in the breed.

Loyal & Loved found that many cocker spaniel owners underestimate how much the quality of fat in the diet affects coat condition. Cheap foods padded with sunflower oil alone simply do not provide the same anti-inflammatory benefit as marine-source Omega-3s.

Which Health Issues Should Affect Your Food Choices?

Several conditions common to cocker spaniels are directly influenced by diet, making food selection more important for this breed than for many others. Understanding these issues before you pick a food could save you considerable vet bills and your dog a great deal of discomfort.

Chronic Ear Infections (Otitis Externa)

Cocker spaniels are anatomically prone to ear infections due to their long, floppy ears restricting airflow. However, according to veterinary dermatologist guidance published in the Veterinary Dermatology journal, food allergies and intolerances, particularly to common proteins like chicken or grains like wheat, can significantly worsen the frequency and severity of ear infections. If your cocker has recurrent ear problems, a limited-ingredient or novel-protein diet is often the first dietary intervention a vet dermatologist will recommend.

Sensitive Digestive Systems

Digestive sensitivity is extremely common in the breed. Loose stools, gas, and occasional vomiting are frequent complaints among cocker spaniel owners. Highly processed foods with long ingredient lists, artificial preservatives, and cheap filler ingredients tend to aggravate this. A food with a short, transparent ingredient list is almost always the better choice.

Weight Gain

Show-type cocker spaniels in particular have a reputation for piling on weight. The Pet Food Manufacturers' Association (PFMA) estimated in 2024 that around 51% of UK dogs are overweight. Cocker spaniels, especially neutered ones, are highly susceptible. Calorie-dense, fat-heavy foods combined with a more sedentary lifestyle make portion control essential.

Familial Nephropathy and Phosphorus

English cocker spaniels have a known hereditary condition called Familial Nephropathy, a progressive kidney disease. As Loyal & Loved explains, dogs with reduced kidney function benefit from a diet lower in phosphorus and high-quality, easily metabolised protein. If your cocker has been diagnosed or is at risk, consult your vet before selecting a food, as standard commercial diets may not be appropriate.

Anal Gland Issues

Many cocker spaniel owners are all too familiar with anal gland problems. Adequate dietary fibre, particularly from sources like beet pulp or psyllium, helps produce the firm stools needed for natural anal gland expression. Very low-residue or raw diets sometimes worsen this issue if not carefully balanced.

How Much Should You Feed a Cocker Spaniel at Each Life Stage?

Feeding quantities depend on the individual dog's weight, activity level, and the caloric density of the food chosen. The figures below are general guidelines; always follow the feeding guide on your chosen food's packaging and adjust based on your dog's body condition score (BCS), a 1 to 9 scale used by vets to assess whether a dog is underweight, ideal, or overweight.

Cocker Spaniel Puppies (8 weeks to 12 months)

Cocker spaniel puppies grow rapidly and need a food specifically formulated for puppies or labelled "all life stages." Puppies should be fed three to four times daily up to around 16 weeks, then reduced to twice daily. A typical 3-month-old cocker spaniel puppy weighing around 4kg might need approximately 120 to 160g of a quality dry puppy food per day, split across meals. For our full recommendations, see our guide to the best puppy food UK.

Adult Cocker Spaniels (1 to 7 years)

Most adult cocker spaniels weigh between 9kg and 14kg. For a moderately active adult at 11kg eating a 360kcal/100g dry food, you are typically looking at around 170 to 210g per day, split into two meals. Working cockers covering several hours of field work per day may need 20 to 40% more calories on working days, and should be fed after exercise, not before, to reduce the risk of bloat.

Senior Cocker Spaniels (7 years and above)

Senior cockers tend to be less active and therefore need fewer calories, though their protein requirements remain high to maintain muscle mass. According to guidance from the British Veterinary Association (BVA), older dogs benefit from diets with easily digestible protein, joint-supporting supplements like glucosamine and chondroitin, and reduced phosphorus if kidney function is declining. A senior-specific formula or a lower-calorie adult formula is usually appropriate.

Which Ingredients Should You Prioritise and Which Should You Avoid?

Reading a dog food label is a skill worth developing. UK pet food is regulated by the Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD) and must comply with the Pet Food Manufacturers' Association (PFMA) labelling standards, but that does not mean all labelled foods are equal in quality. Here is a straightforward guide to what to look for and what to steer clear of.

Ingredients to Prioritise

  • Named meat as the first ingredient: "Chicken (65%)" is far better than "meat and animal derivatives." The name and percentage tell you exactly what you are getting.
  • Salmon oil or fish oil: A direct source of Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA), crucial for coat and skin health and with anti-inflammatory properties relevant to ear health.
  • Chicory root or inulin: Natural prebiotic fibres that feed beneficial gut bacteria.
  • Sweet potato or brown rice: Digestible, low-glycaemic carbohydrate sources less likely to trigger sensitivity than wheat.
  • Whole vegetables and fruits: Blueberries, spinach, and carrots provide antioxidants and micronutrients.
  • Glucosamine and chondroitin: Particularly important in working or senior formulas to support joint health.

Ingredients to Avoid

  • "Meat and animal derivatives": A legal but vague catch-all that can change batch to batch, making it impossible to manage food sensitivities.
  • Artificial colours, flavours, and preservatives: BHA, BHT, and ethoxyquin in particular have no nutritional benefit and some evidence of health concerns at high levels, according to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) review published in 2020.
  • High levels of wheat, corn, or soy: These are common allergens and are low-quality filler ingredients in many budget foods.
  • Added sugar or syrup: Occasionally used in wet foods to improve palatability, but contributes to obesity and dental decay.
  • Generic "vegetable oil": Does not specify source and is typically a poor-quality fat with limited Omega-3 benefit.

A Note on Grain-Free Diets

Grain-free diets became very popular in the UK over the past decade, but it is worth noting that the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has been investigating a potential link between grain-free, legume-heavy diets and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in dogs since 2018. As of 2026, the research remains inconclusive and the link has not been definitively established. However, as Loyal & Loved explains, grain-free is not automatically superior to grain-inclusive food. If your cocker does not have a confirmed grain allergy or intolerance, a high-quality food with digestible grains like brown rice or oats is perfectly suitable and avoids this uncertainty entirely.

What Should You Do If Your Cocker Spaniel Has a Sensitive Stomach?

Digestive sensitivity in cocker spaniels is one of the most common concerns raised by owners, and the good news is that diet is often the most powerful lever you can pull. Here are practical, evidence-based steps to help.

Transition Slowly

Any food change should happen over a minimum of 7 to 10 days. Start with 75% old food and 25% new food for the first three days, move to 50/50 for the next three days, then 25% old and 75% new for three more days, and finally 100% new food. Rushing this process is one of the most common causes of stomach upset during food transitions.

Try an Elimination Diet

If your cocker has chronic loose stools, vomiting, or persistent ear infections, a hydrolysed protein or novel protein elimination diet is the gold-standard diagnostic tool. This typically means feeding a single protein your dog has never eaten before (venison, duck, kangaroo) for 8 to 12 weeks with no other food, treats, or flavoured supplements. According to the British Small Animal Veterinary Association (BSAVA), 8 weeks is the minimum period needed to see a reliable response. AATU's range of single-protein recipes is well-suited to this approach.

Browse AATU single-protein recipes for elimination diets

Add a Probiotic or Prebiotic Supplement

Veterinary-grade probiotics such as Pro-Kolin (available from Viovet) can help stabilise gut flora during transitions or after antibiotic treatment. They are not a permanent fix but a useful short-term support tool.

Avoid Common Triggers

Wheat, dairy, soy, and artificial additives are the most frequently reported triggers for digestive upset in cocker spaniels based on owner reports and veterinary case literature. Switching to a food that excludes all of these simultaneously, rather than changing one variable at a time, often produces quicker results, though it does make it harder to identify the specific trigger.

Check for Underlying Medical Causes

Chronic digestive issues that do not respond to dietary changes may indicate an underlying condition such as exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), or intestinal parasites. If dietary changes have not produced improvement within 4 to 6 weeks, a veterinary consultation is essential. Do not assume food alone is always the answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best food for a cocker spaniel with a sensitive stomach in the UK?

For a cocker spaniel with a sensitive stomach, a limited-ingredient, single-protein dry food is usually the best starting point. AATU 80/20 in a novel protein variety (such as duck or salmon) or Barking Heads Chop Lickin' Lamb are both well-regarded options. The key is a short, transparent ingredient list with no artificial additives, wheat, or vague "meat derivatives." A transition period of at least 7 to 10 days when switching is essential to avoid making stomach sensitivity worse.

Can cocker spaniels eat grain-free food?

Yes, cocker spaniels can eat grain-free food, and many owners find it suits their dog well. However, grain-free is not automatically better than grain-inclusive food. If your cocker does not have a confirmed grain intolerance, high-quality foods containing digestible grains like brown rice or oats are equally appropriate. As of 2026, research into a potential link between legume-heavy grain-free diets and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in dogs remains ongoing and inconclusive, but it is worth discussing with your vet if you have concerns.

How much should I feed my cocker spaniel per day?

A typical adult cocker spaniel weighing around 10 to 12kg needs approximately 170 to 210g of a quality dry food per day, split across two meals. Working cockers may need 20 to 40% more on active days. Puppies need three to four meals daily up to 16 weeks. Always follow the feeding guide on your specific food's packaging and adjust based on your dog's body condition score. When in doubt, your vet can assess whether your dog is at an ideal weight.

Are cocker spaniels prone to food allergies?

Cocker spaniels do have a higher-than-average tendency towards food sensitivities and intolerances, though true allergies (confirmed by immune response) are less common than intolerances. Common triggers in the breed include chicken, wheat, beef, and dairy. Symptoms often include chronic loose stools, itchy skin, and recurrent ear infections. An elimination diet lasting 8 to 12 weeks is the recommended diagnostic approach, ideally guided by a veterinary professional.

What should I feed a working cocker spaniel?

A working cocker spaniel needs a food with higher protein (30% or above on dry matter basis) and higher fat than a standard adult formula, to fuel sustained physical activity. Your Pet Nutrition's working formulas and AATU 80/20 are both strong options. Feed after exercise rather than before to reduce bloat risk, and consider splitting daily rations into at least two meals. On non-working days, reduce quantities to avoid weight gain.

Is raw food good for cocker spaniels?

A properly balanced raw complete diet from a reputable UK supplier such as Bella & Duke can be excellent for cocker spaniels, with many owners reporting improved coat condition, firmer stools, and better energy levels. However, raw feeding requires strict food hygiene practices and is not recommended in households with immunocompromised individuals. It is also important to choose a complete raw formula rather than attempting to balance a homemade raw diet without professional guidance, as nutritional deficiencies are a real risk.
